Long-Term Care Insurance: Costs, Coverage & How It Works

Long-term care insurance can help pay for ongoing assistance with everyday activities when you can no longer live fully independently. Learn how LTC insurance works, what it covers, how benefits are triggered, what policies can cost, and how to compare coverage options.

Quick answer Long-term care insurance is designed to help pay for services such as personal care at home, assisted living, adult day care, or nursing-home care. Policies differ significantly in their benefit amounts, eligibility requirements, waiting periods, inflation protection, exclusions, and premium structure.

What Is Long-Term Care?

Long-term care is assistance or supervision that helps a person manage everyday activities when an illness, injury, disability, cognitive impairment, or aging-related condition makes independent living difficult.

Long-term care is different from treatment for a temporary medical condition. It often focuses on personal and custodial assistance, such as bathing, dressing, eating, transferring, toileting, or managing incontinence.

Care can be provided at home, in an adult day care setting, in assisted living, or in a nursing home. Some people need care for a relatively short period, while others may need assistance for several years.

What Is Long-Term Care Insurance?

Long-term care insurance is insurance designed to help pay for eligible long-term care services. Depending on the policy, benefits may cover home care, assisted living, adult day care, nursing-home care, or several types of care.

You generally pay premiums in exchange for a pool of benefits that can be used if you satisfy the policy's requirements for receiving care.

Unlike ordinary health insurance, LTC insurance is primarily concerned with assistance with daily living and long-term support rather than only treating an acute medical condition.

Home care

Depending on the policy, benefits may help pay for qualifying personal care, homemaker services, home health care, or other covered services at home.

Assisted living

Some policies provide benefits for assisted living facilities and other residential care settings.

Adult day care

Certain policies cover eligible adult day health or community-based care.

Nursing-home care

Policies may provide benefits for skilled, intermediate, or custodial care depending on the contract.

Why Long-Term Care Planning Matters

Long-term care can become a significant retirement expense. Fidelity's July 2026 analysis cites Department of Health and Human Services research indicating that someone turning 65 today has nearly a 70% chance of needing some type of long-term care during the remainder of life.

The need for care can also affect family members. When care is provided at home, relatives may provide unpaid assistance, reduce working hours, or help coordinate professional services.

Planning does not necessarily mean buying insurance. It means deciding how you would pay for care and what type of care you would want if you could no longer perform certain everyday activities independently.

Where Can Long-Term Care Be Provided?

Long-term care can take place in several different settings. The right setting depends on a person's health, level of independence, finances, family support, and personal preferences.

Setting Typical purpose Examples of care
Home Remain in your own residence Personal care, meal assistance, homemaker services, home health services
Adult day care Daytime supervision and support Personal assistance, activities, supervision, selected health services
Assisted living Housing combined with ongoing assistance Meals, medication assistance, personal care, supervision
Nursing home More intensive ongoing care Skilled, intermediate, or custodial care depending on the facility and resident's needs

How Does Long-Term Care Insurance Work?

LTC insurance generally works by establishing a benefit amount and conditions that must be met before benefits become available.

  1. You apply for coverage. The insurer may ask health questions and perform medical underwriting.
  2. You choose policy features. These can include the benefit amount, benefit duration, elimination period, and inflation protection.
  3. You pay premiums. Premiums depend on factors such as age, health, coverage amount, policy features, and insurer.
  4. You become eligible for benefits. You must satisfy the policy's benefit trigger requirements.
  5. You receive covered care. The policy pays or reimburses eligible expenses according to its terms.

Many policies use a reimbursement model, meaning eligible expenses are incurred and submitted for payment. Some policies may instead provide a cash benefit. The actual claims process depends on the policy contract.

Long-Term Care Insurance Benefit Triggers

A major part of an LTC policy is the definition of when you qualify for benefits.

Many policies use an inability to perform at least two of six activities of daily living, commonly called ADLs, or a qualifying cognitive impairment. Exact definitions and claim requirements depend on the policy.

Bathing

Getting clean and maintaining personal hygiene without assistance.

Dressing

Putting on and removing ordinary clothing and necessary accessories.

Eating

Feeding oneself after food has been prepared.

Toileting

Getting to and using the toilet and maintaining related personal hygiene.

Transferring

Moving between locations such as a bed and a chair.

Continence

Managing bladder and bowel functions and related personal care.

Important: Do not assume that every LTC policy uses exactly the same trigger. Read the policy's definition of disability, cognitive impairment, ADLs, and certification requirements.

Important Long-Term Care Insurance Features

Daily or monthly benefit

A policy may specify the maximum amount it will pay per day or month for covered care. A higher benefit generally provides more protection but can also increase premiums.

Benefit period

The benefit period determines how long the policy can provide benefits. Some policies specify a number of years, while others structure coverage as a total pool of available benefits.

Maximum benefit pool

Think of the maximum benefit as the pool of money available under the policy. The actual amount available depends on the policy's benefit formula, limits, and claims.

Elimination period

The elimination period is the period you must satisfy after becoming eligible before the policy begins paying under its terms. Common examples include 30, 60, or 90 days, but policies vary.

Inflation protection

Long-term care costs can rise substantially over time. Inflation protection is designed to increase benefits so the coverage does not become outdated as care costs increase.

Inflation protection can substantially affect the premium, so compare the initial benefit, future benefit increases, and total premium cost rather than looking only at the starting price.

Care location

Check whether the policy covers care at home, assisted living, adult day care, nursing homes, or other settings you would realistically consider.

Reimbursement vs. cash benefits

Some policies reimburse eligible expenses, while others may offer cash benefits subject to their contract terms. Understand how claims are paid before choosing coverage.

How Much Does Long-Term Care Cost?

Long-term care costs vary substantially by location, type of care, provider, and amount of assistance required.

Fidelity's July 2026 article reports the following national median annual costs for 2025:

Type of care 2025 national median annual cost
In-home caregiving $80,080
Adult day health care $34,675
Assisted living facility $74,400
Nursing home — semi-private room $114,972
Nursing home — private room $129,575

These are national median figures, not a quote for any particular person or location. Actual costs can be considerably different by state and metropolitan area.

Planning tip: When estimating future costs, do not compare your policy only with today's price. Consider how care costs could change over the years and whether your policy includes inflation protection.

Ways to Pay for Long-Term Care

Long-term care does not have to be funded through one source. Your eventual strategy may combine insurance, savings, retirement assets, government programs, and family resources.

Long-term care insurance

Insurance can transfer some of the financial risk of future care expenses to an insurer, subject to the policy's limits and requirements.

Personal savings

Self-funding gives you flexibility but places the financial risk of care costs directly on your retirement assets.

Hybrid insurance

Some life insurance or annuity products combine long-term care benefits with another financial benefit.

Government programs

Depending on eligibility and circumstances, Medicaid, veterans programs, or state programs may help with certain long-term care expenses.

Medicare vs. Medicaid for Long-Term Care

Medicare and Medicaid are often confused when people plan for long-term care, but they serve different purposes and have different eligibility rules.

Does Medicare cover long-term care?

Medicare generally does not pay for long-term custodial care. Medicare.gov explains that most long-term care is non-medical and is not covered by Medicare or Medigap.

Medicare can cover certain skilled or medically necessary services in specific circumstances, but that should not be confused with broad long-term custodial care.

Does Medicaid cover long-term care?

Medicaid can help eligible people with long-term services and supports, but eligibility and covered services depend on federal and state rules.

Because Medicaid is generally means-tested, people may need to meet financial and other eligibility requirements. Planning should account for the rules in the state where you live.

Key distinction: Medicare eligibility does not automatically mean that long-term care will be paid for. Medicaid can provide long-term care assistance for eligible individuals, but it is not simply another form of Medicare.

Traditional vs. Hybrid Long-Term Care Insurance

Feature Traditional LTC insurance Hybrid policy
Main purpose Long-term care protection Long-term care combined with another benefit
Other benefit Usually no life insurance death benefit Often life insurance or annuity benefits
Premium structure Varies by policy Can include periodic premiums or other funding structures
If care is never needed Depends on policy; generally there is no traditional death benefit A life insurance or annuity benefit may remain depending on the contract
Complexity Primarily focused on LTC benefits Can involve both insurance and financial-product considerations

Hybrid products can be attractive to some consumers because they combine benefits, but they can also be more complex. Review the policy carefully and understand guarantees, fees, surrender provisions, death benefits, long-term care benefits, and tax considerations before purchasing.

When Should You Consider Long-Term Care Insurance?

There is no single age that is right for everyone. The decision depends on your health, income, assets, retirement plan, family situation, and ability to comfortably maintain premiums.

Fidelity notes that people often consider LTC insurance in their 50s while reviewing their retirement plans. Waiting can increase the cost of coverage and may make qualifying for coverage more difficult if health changes.

That does not mean everyone should buy coverage at a particular age. The more useful question is whether the policy fits your financial plan and whether you can afford the premiums over the long term.

Questions to consider

  • How would I pay for several years of care?
  • How much of my retirement savings could be used without disrupting my retirement plan?
  • Would I want to receive care at home?
  • Would my family be able to provide unpaid care?
  • Could I comfortably maintain the premiums if they increase?
  • How much protection would I need against rising care costs?

How to Compare Long-Term Care Insurance Policies

Comparing policies based only on the monthly or annual premium can be misleading. Two policies with similar prices can provide very different amounts of protection.

  • Compare the daily or monthly benefit.
  • Compare the maximum benefit pool and benefit duration.
  • Check whether home care is covered.
  • Check assisted living and nursing-home coverage.
  • Understand the ADL and cognitive impairment benefit triggers.
  • Compare elimination periods.
  • Compare inflation protection options.
  • Ask whether premiums are guaranteed or may increase.
  • Review exclusions and limitations.
  • Check the insurer's financial strength and claims-paying ability.
  • Request an outline of coverage and sample policy documents.
  • Compare multiple insurers using equivalent benefit assumptions.

The California Department of Insurance specifically recommends reviewing policy benefits, premium history, insurer information, and policy comparison materials when evaluating LTC coverage in California.

California Long-Term Care Insurance

California has additional consumer protections and rules for long-term care insurance. These rules should not be treated as nationwide requirements.

The California Department of Insurance describes three categories of LTC policies sold under California rules: Nursing Facility and Residential Care Facility Only, Home Care Only, and Comprehensive Long-Term Care.

California also has the California Partnership for Long-Term Care, a state program involving approved insurers and additional policy requirements. Partnership policies can include inflation protection and an asset-protection feature related to future Medi-Cal eligibility, subject to the program's rules.

California consumers can also receive free counseling through the Health Insurance Counseling and Advocacy Program, commonly called HICAP. California Health Advocates identifies HICAP as a resource for understanding and comparing long-term care insurance.

California reminder: State-specific insurance requirements can change. If you live in California, review the current California Department of Insurance guidance and the actual policy documents before purchasing coverage.

Common Long-Term Care Insurance Mistakes

Focusing only on the premium

A lower premium may come with lower benefits, shorter coverage, a longer elimination period, or weaker inflation protection. Compare the entire benefit structure.

Ignoring inflation

A benefit that appears adequate today may provide substantially less purchasing power decades later. Consider how inflation protection changes future benefits and premiums.

Assuming Medicare will pay

Medicare is not a general solution for long-term custodial care. Understanding this distinction is important when building a retirement plan.

Buying more coverage than you can maintain

An expensive policy is not necessarily useful if the premiums become unaffordable. Coverage should fit your long-term financial capacity.

Not checking the benefit trigger

The amount of insurance is only part of the policy. Understand exactly when you become eligible to receive benefits.

Not comparing insurers

Premiums and policy features can differ among insurers. Compare equivalent coverage rather than comparing prices for completely different policies.

Long-Term Care Planning Without Insurance

Long-term care planning can still be useful even if you decide not to purchase LTC insurance.

  • Estimate potential care costs in your area.
  • Review retirement income and investment assets.
  • Build an emergency reserve appropriate for your overall financial situation.
  • Consider where you would prefer to receive care.
  • Discuss caregiving expectations with family members.
  • Review powers of attorney and other estate planning documents with appropriate professionals.
  • Learn about Medicaid rules in your state.
  • Revisit the plan as your health, finances, and family circumstances change.

Long-term care planning is ultimately broader than insurance. AARP similarly describes LTC planning as involving home, health, finances, and future care preferences rather than simply purchasing an insurance policy.

Frequently Asked Questions About Long-Term Care Insurance

What is long-term care insurance?

Long-term care insurance helps pay for eligible services that assist with everyday activities when a person cannot live fully independently. Depending on the policy, coverage may include home care, assisted living, adult day care, or nursing-home care.

Does Medicare cover long-term care?

Medicare generally does not cover long-term custodial care. It can cover certain skilled services in specific circumstances, but it should not be treated as comprehensive long-term care insurance.

Does Medicaid pay for long-term care?

Medicaid can pay for certain long-term services for people who meet applicable eligibility requirements. Rules differ by state and can include financial and functional eligibility requirements.

What are activities of daily living?

Activities of daily living, or ADLs, commonly include bathing, dressing, eating, toileting, transferring, and continence. Many LTC policies use inability to perform a specified number of ADLs as part of the benefit trigger.

What is an elimination period?

An elimination period is the period that must be satisfied after you become eligible for benefits before the policy begins paying according to its terms. Policies differ in how the period is measured and handled.

Can long-term care insurance premiums increase?

Some policies allow premiums to increase subject to applicable state requirements and regulatory approval. Before buying coverage, ask whether premiums are guaranteed and review the insurer's premium history.

What is inflation protection?

Inflation protection is a policy feature designed to increase benefits over time so that the coverage can better keep pace with rising care costs. The specific increase method and cost depend on the policy.

Is long-term care insurance worth it?

There is no universal answer. The decision depends on your health, assets, retirement income, family circumstances, desired care, ability to maintain premiums, and tolerance for the financial risk of paying for care yourself.

When should I consider long-term care insurance?

Many people consider coverage in their 50s or 60s while reviewing retirement plans. Applying earlier can sometimes mean lower premiums or easier underwriting, but the appropriate timing depends on the individual's circumstances.

What is a hybrid long-term care policy?

A hybrid policy combines long-term care benefits with another insurance or financial benefit, commonly life insurance or an annuity. The contract determines how the benefits interact.
