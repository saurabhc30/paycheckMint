MONTHLY BUDGETING

Monthly Budget

Create a realistic monthly budget by organizing your income, bills, everyday expenses, savings and debt payments in one simple plan.

What is a monthly budget?

A monthly budget is a plan for how you will use the money you expect to receive during a month. It organizes income into categories such as housing, food, transportation, insurance, debt, savings and personal spending.

A monthly budget gives you a clearer picture of where your money is going. Instead of looking at individual bills or purchases separately, you can see your income and major spending categories together.

A useful monthly budget does not need to be identical every month. Income, utility bills, groceries, transportation, medical expenses and other costs can change. The goal is to create a plan, compare it with actual spending and make adjustments when necessary.

What Is a Monthly Budget?

A monthly budget is a spending and savings plan covering one month. It starts with the money available to you and assigns that money to different financial categories.

Monthly Income − Monthly Expenses = Money Remaining

Your budget can also include savings and investing as planned allocations. This helps you see how much money is available after your regular commitments.

Why Use a Monthly Budget?

A monthly budget can help you understand your cash flow and identify areas where your actual spending differs from your plan.

  • See where your money is going.
  • Plan upcoming bills.
  • Set aside money for savings.
  • Include debt payments in your plan.
  • Prepare for irregular expenses.
  • Identify categories where spending can change.
  • Compare planned spending with actual spending.

Monthly Budget Categories

The categories in a monthly budget should reflect your actual household spending. You do not have to use every possible category, and some people may need additional categories.

Housing

Rent or mortgage, property-related costs, renters or homeowners insurance and other housing expenses.

Utilities

Electricity, natural gas, water, internet, phone and other household services.

Food

Groceries, restaurants, takeout and other food purchases.

Transportation

Car payments, fuel, insurance, maintenance, parking and public transportation.

Health Care

Health insurance, medical expenses, dental costs, prescriptions and other health-related spending.

Debt

Credit cards, student loans, auto loans and other required debt payments.

Savings

Emergency savings, short-term goals, retirement and other planned savings.

Personal & Entertainment

Clothing, personal care, hobbies, subscriptions, entertainment and other discretionary costs.

Fixed vs. Variable Monthly Expenses

Separating fixed and variable expenses can make your monthly budget easier to understand.

Fixed Expenses

Fixed expenses are generally predictable and may stay relatively similar from month to month.

  • Rent or mortgage
  • Car payment
  • Insurance premiums
  • Minimum debt payments
  • Some subscriptions

Variable Expenses

Variable expenses can change depending on usage, purchases or circumstances.

  • Groceries
  • Gas
  • Utilities
  • Dining out
  • Entertainment
  • Clothing
  • Home maintenance

Don't Forget Irregular Expenses

Some costs do not arrive every month but still need to be part of your overall financial plan.

  • Car repairs
  • Home repairs
  • Holiday gifts
  • Annual insurance bills
  • Property taxes
  • Travel
  • School expenses
  • Annual memberships
  • Medical expenses

One simple method is to estimate an annual cost and divide it by 12. You can then reserve that amount each month for the future expense.

Example:

If you expect to spend $1,200 on holiday gifts during the year, setting aside $100 per month creates a planned category instead of waiting for the expense to arrive.

What Income Should You Use for a Monthly Budget?

For a household spending budget, start with money actually available to spend rather than simply using your annual salary.

For employees, this generally means using take-home pay after payroll deductions. Other reliable income sources may also be included.

If you are paid weekly, biweekly or semimonthly, convert your income carefully rather than assuming every month has the same number of paychecks.

For variable income, consider using a conservative estimate and adjusting the budget when actual income is known.

Monthly Budget vs. Paycheck Budget

A monthly budget organizes your overall financial plan for the month. A paycheck budget focuses on when money actually arrives.

Combining the two can be useful. Your monthly budget can define your total spending limits, while your paycheck plan can determine when to set money aside for bills and goals.

See the Paycheck Budgeting guide for more information.

How to Include Savings in a Monthly Budget

Savings can be treated as a planned part of your monthly allocation rather than whatever happens to remain after spending.

Common savings goals include:

  • Emergency fund
  • Short-term purchases
  • Travel
  • Home expenses
  • Vehicle expenses
  • Education
  • Retirement
  • Other financial goals

How to Include Debt in a Monthly Budget

Include required debt payments in your monthly plan. This may include credit cards, student loans, auto loans and personal loans.

After required payments are accounted for, your available cash flow can help determine whether additional debt payments fit into your plan.

What If Your Monthly Budget Does Not Balance?

If your planned expenses are greater than your monthly income, your budget has a shortfall.

Start by reviewing your categories rather than immediately making arbitrary cuts.

  • Check for forgotten or duplicated expenses.
  • Review subscriptions and recurring charges.
  • Examine dining and entertainment spending.
  • Review transportation costs.
  • Check whether irregular expenses are included.
  • Verify your income estimate.
  • Consider whether additional income is realistic.

If essential expenses already exceed available income, the solution may require looking at larger recurring costs or income rather than only small discretionary purchases.

How to Track a Monthly Budget

A budget is a plan, while tracking shows what actually happened.

During the month, record purchases and payments in the appropriate categories. At the end of the month, compare actual spending with your planned amounts.

  1. Record income.
  2. Record expenses.
  3. Categorize transactions.
  4. Compare actual spending with planned spending.
  5. Identify categories that changed significantly.
  6. Update next month's budget.

How Often Should You Review Your Monthly Budget?

Check your budget throughout the month if you are actively trying to control spending. At minimum, review the completed month before creating the next month's plan.

You should also update your budget when something important changes, such as a new job, raise, rent change, new debt payment, insurance change or major household expense.

Monthly Budget and the 50/30/20 Rule

The 50/30/20 rule is one way to organize a budget by grouping after-tax income into needs, wants and savings or debt payments.

It can be useful as a starting framework, but actual expenses vary significantly between households. A detailed monthly budget allows you to see your own categories rather than relying only on broad percentages.

Read the 50/30/20 Budget Rule guide to learn more.

Monthly Budget and Zero-Based Budgeting

Zero-based budgeting gives planned income a specific purpose through expenses, savings, debt payments or other allocations.

A monthly budget can be simple and flexible, while a zero-based budget generally provides a more detailed allocation of available income.

Learn more in the Zero-Based Budgeting guide .

Common Monthly Budget Mistakes

Forgetting irregular expenses

Annual bills, repairs, gifts and other occasional expenses can disrupt a budget when they are ignored.

Using unrealistic numbers

A budget based on spending amounts that are far below your actual behavior may be difficult to maintain.

Leaving out savings

If savings are important to your goals, include them as a planned category rather than relying entirely on whatever remains at the end of the month.

Not reviewing actual spending

Your budget is an estimate. Comparing it with actual transactions helps make future budgets more realistic.

A Simple Monthly Budget Process

  1. Calculate monthly take-home income.
  2. List housing and essential bills.
  3. Add food and transportation.
  4. Add insurance and health costs.
  5. Add debt payments.
  6. Plan savings and investing.
  7. Add personal and discretionary spending.
  8. Account for irregular expenses.
  9. Compare planned expenses with income.
  10. Track actual spending and update the next month.
