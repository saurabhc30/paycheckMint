Budget After a Raise: How to Adjust Your Budget

Getting a raise increases your earning power, but the amount added to your checking account may be smaller than the headline salary increase. Learn how to calculate the difference, update your budget, avoid lifestyle inflation, and put your extra take-home pay to work.

What should you do after getting a raise? First, find out how much additional take-home pay the raise actually creates. Then update your budget for taxes, benefits and any new work-related expenses. Decide how much of the increase should go toward savings, debt, financial goals, necessary expenses and lifestyle spending.

Update your budget Replace your old income with the new take-home amount and review recurring expenses.

What Changes When You Get a Raise?

A raise changes more than the number on your salary offer. Your paycheck may change, your payroll deductions may change, and a promotion can introduce new work-related costs.

The first number to identify is your new take-home pay. A salary increase is generally discussed in gross dollars, while your budget depends on the money actually available after payroll taxes and deductions.

New Take-Home Pay − Old Take-Home Pay = Additional Take-Home Pay

Once you know the additional amount, you can give those dollars a specific job instead of allowing them to disappear into everyday spending.

Step 1: Find Out How Much of the Raise You Actually Keep

Suppose your monthly gross salary increases by $500. Your checking account will not necessarily receive an additional $500 because payroll taxes and other deductions may reduce the increase.

This is one reason a raise can feel smaller than expected. The advertised increase and the increase in take-home pay are different numbers.

Check your first paycheck after the raise and compare it with a previous paycheck. Look at:

  • Gross pay
  • Federal income tax withholding
  • State and local tax withholding where applicable
  • Social Security and Medicare taxes
  • Health insurance deductions
  • Retirement contributions
  • Other payroll deductions
  • Net pay

If you want to estimate your paycheck before the new pay starts, use the Paycheck Calculator.

Step 2: Update Your Existing Budget

You don't necessarily need to create a completely new budget after every raise. Start with your existing budget and update the income number.

  1. Replace your old take-home income with the new amount.
  2. Update taxes and payroll-related deductions if necessary.
  3. Add any new work-related expenses.
  4. Review recurring subscriptions and lifestyle costs.
  5. Increase savings or debt goals intentionally.
  6. Decide how much additional discretionary spending you want.
  7. Check how much remains unassigned.

A raise can also be an opportunity to rebuild a budget that you have outgrown. If your old budget was created several years ago, your housing, transportation, insurance, family or debt costs may have changed substantially.

Step 3: Look for New Expenses Caused by the Raise

A promotion can increase expenses as well as income. For example, a new position could require a longer commute, more office clothing, professional travel or additional meals away from home.

These costs should be considered when you calculate the real financial effect of the promotion.

Possible Change Budget Impact
Longer commute Fuel, parking, transit or vehicle maintenance
More office days Meals, clothing and commuting expenses
Business travel Potential personal spending around travel
Higher benefits contribution Reduced take-home pay
Higher retirement contribution More savings but less current take-home pay

Step 4: Watch for Lifestyle Inflation

Lifestyle inflation, sometimes called lifestyle creep, occurs when spending increases as income increases.

A raise might make a more expensive apartment, newer vehicle, additional subscriptions or more frequent restaurant meals seem affordable. Any of these choices can be reasonable if they fit the new budget. The problem occurs when recurring expenses automatically consume most or all of the additional income.

One useful question is: “If I hadn't received this raise, would I still consider this expense necessary?”

That question can help separate an actual need from an upgrade that simply became possible because income increased.

Step 5: Give Your Raise a Purpose

There is no single correct percentage for allocating a raise. Your priorities may be different from another person's.

Possible destinations for additional take-home pay include:

  • Emergency savings
  • Retirement contributions
  • High-interest debt payments
  • Home down-payment savings
  • Education savings
  • Upcoming large purchases
  • Insurance or other necessary expenses
  • Travel and experiences
  • Personal spending

You can also divide the increase. For example, you might send part toward savings, part toward debt and part toward something you enjoy. The important part is deciding the allocation before the extra income gets absorbed into everyday spending.

Step 6: Increase Savings Automatically

One practical way to prevent a raise from becoming automatic lifestyle inflation is to increase savings at the same time your income increases.

For example, if your monthly take-home pay increases by $350, you could schedule an additional transfer to savings rather than waiting to see what remains at the end of the month.

Automatic contributions can be useful for goals such as:

  • Emergency savings
  • Retirement
  • Home purchase
  • Vehicle replacement
  • Education
  • Annual expenses

The amount should fit your overall financial situation. Increasing savings is not the only legitimate use of a raise.

Step 7: Consider Paying Down High-Interest Debt

A raise can create additional room for debt payments. If you carry high-interest debt, putting some additional cash toward the balance may reduce the amount of interest that accumulates over time.

First make sure required minimum payments are included in your normal budget. Then decide whether extra payments fit your priorities.

Once a debt is paid off, the amount previously used for that payment can be redirected toward another goal instead of becoming automatic lifestyle spending.

Step 8: Review Retirement Contributions

A raise can also be an opportunity to review how much you're contributing toward retirement.

If your employer offers a workplace retirement plan, review your contribution rate and any available employer matching rules. You may decide to increase your contribution rather than directing the entire raise toward current spending.

Retirement contributions can have tax implications depending on the type of account and your circumstances, so review the specific rules that apply to your plan.

Step 9: Leave Room to Enjoy the Raise

A budget after a raise does not have to mean sending every additional dollar toward savings or debt.

It is reasonable to use part of an increase for things that improve your quality of life if they fit within your budget. The key is to make those choices intentional rather than allowing every recurring expense to rise automatically.

You might use part of a raise for a vacation, hobbies, dining, entertainment, personal purchases or another priority while directing the rest toward longer-term goals.

Budget After a Raise: Example

Imagine someone earning $5,000 per month in gross pay receives a $500 monthly raise. Their new gross income becomes $5,500.

If their estimated combined tax and deduction rate on the additional income were 25%, the estimated extra take-home pay would be about $375 per month.

Use of Extra Take-Home Pay Example Amount
Savings / investing $150
Extra debt payment $75
Lifestyle / flexible spending $75
Other financial goal $75
Total $375

This is only an illustration. The actual amount available from a raise depends on payroll taxes, benefits, retirement contributions and other deductions. The allocation should also reflect the household's actual priorities.

Can You Use the 50/30/20 Budget After a Raise?

The 50/30/20 framework can be used as one way to review how additional income is being allocated. It broadly separates take-home income into needs, wants and savings or debt goals.

However, the percentages are a framework rather than a requirement. Housing, childcare, healthcare, transportation and other essential costs can vary substantially between households.

If you already use this approach, a raise is a natural point to review whether your current percentages still reflect your priorities.

Use Your Raise to Rebuild Your Monthly Budget

A raise is also a useful reason to review your complete monthly budget rather than focusing only on the new income.

Look at housing, utilities, groceries, transportation, insurance, debt, subscriptions, savings and discretionary spending. You may discover that some costs have increased independently of your raise.

A complete monthly budget can show how much of the new income is already committed and how much is actually available for new goals.

Budgeting After a Promotion

A promotion can be different from a simple annual raise because it may change both compensation and responsibilities.

Before treating the entire salary increase as available spending, consider whether the new role changes:

  • Commute distance
  • Transportation costs
  • Work clothing needs
  • Professional travel
  • Meals during the workday
  • Childcare arrangements
  • Health or other employee benefits
  • Retirement contributions

Once these changes are understood, the remaining increase can be incorporated into the new budget.

Common Mistakes After Getting a Raise

Budgeting with the gross raise

A $6,000 annual salary increase does not mean $6,000 of additional spendable cash. Start with the change in take-home pay.

Increasing fixed expenses too quickly

A one-time celebration is different from adding a permanent monthly expense. New housing, car payments and subscriptions can continue long after the excitement of a raise fades.

Ignoring existing goals

If you're already saving for a home, paying off debt or building an emergency fund, a raise can accelerate that goal.

Saving nothing because you want to enjoy the raise

Enjoying part of a raise is reasonable, but allowing all of it to disappear into recurring spending means your financial goals may not benefit from the higher income.

Saving everything and creating an unrealistic budget

The opposite extreme can also be difficult to maintain. A sustainable budget should reflect both future goals and current quality of life.

Budget After a Raise Checklist

  1. Confirm your new gross salary.
  2. Check your first paycheck at the new salary.
  3. Calculate the actual increase in take-home pay.
  4. Review taxes and payroll deductions.
  5. Identify any new work-related expenses.
  6. Update your monthly budget.
  7. Decide how much to increase savings.
  8. Review debt-payment opportunities.
  9. Review retirement contributions.
  10. Set a reasonable amount for lifestyle spending.
  11. Automate the financial goals you choose.
  12. Review the budget again after a few months.

Budget After a Raise FAQ

How should I budget after getting a raise?

Start by determining how much additional take-home pay the raise creates. Then update your budget for taxes, benefits and any new work-related expenses. Decide how much of the increase should go toward savings, debt, financial goals and lifestyle spending.

Should I save my entire raise?

You do not necessarily have to save the entire raise. You can divide the additional take-home pay among savings, debt reduction, necessary expenses and discretionary spending based on your goals and circumstances.

Why does my raise feel smaller than expected?

A raise is normally quoted as gross pay before taxes and payroll deductions. The increase in take-home pay can therefore be smaller than the increase in gross income.

What is lifestyle inflation after a raise?

Lifestyle inflation occurs when spending increases as income increases. Examples include moving into more expensive housing, upgrading a vehicle, eating out more frequently or adding recurring subscriptions.

When should I update my budget after a raise?

Update your budget when the new pay actually begins and you know the effect on take-home pay. You should also review the budget if the promotion creates new expenses or changes taxes, benefits, debt or other recurring costs.

What should I do with extra money from a raise?

Possible uses include emergency savings, paying down high-interest debt, increasing retirement contributions, saving for a specific goal, covering higher necessary expenses, or allocating some money to discretionary spending.
