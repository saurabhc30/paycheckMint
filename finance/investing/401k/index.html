401(k) Plans: Build Retirement Savings One Paycheck at a Time

A 401(k) can turn regular payroll contributions into long-term retirement savings. Learn how contributions, employer matching, traditional and Roth options, vesting, investment choices and fees fit together.

What Is a 401(k)?

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement plan that lets eligible employees save and invest money through payroll deductions.

Instead of manually transferring money after every paycheck, your contribution is typically taken directly from your paycheck and deposited into your retirement account. The money can then be invested in the options offered by your employer's plan.

💵
Payroll contributions

You choose a contribution amount or percentage and contributions are generally made automatically from your paycheck.

🏢
Employer contributions

Some employers add matching or other contributions according to the plan's rules.

📈
Invested for retirement

Your account can hold investments selected from the choices available in your employer's plan.

How Does a 401(k) Work?

A typical 401(k) starts with a decision about how much of your paycheck you want to contribute. Your employer's plan then directs those contributions into your 401(k) account.

You generally select investments from the choices available through the plan. Over time, additional contributions and investment returns can change the account balance.

Some employers also contribute money. Employer contributions may be subject to a vesting schedule, meaning the amount you are entitled to keep if you leave the company can depend on how long you have worked there and the plan's rules.

1

Your contribution is not automatically the same thing as an investment. You normally need to select investments from the options offered by the plan.

5

Review your contribution rate, investments, fees, beneficiaries and plan changes as your circumstances change.

What Is a 401(k) Employer Match?

An employer match is money an employer contributes to your retirement account based on your own contributions. The exact formula varies by employer.

For example, a plan might contribute a specified amount for each dollar you contribute, up to a particular percentage of eligible compensation. Another employer may use a different formula.

Illustrative example:

Suppose an employee contributes $300 per month and the employer contributes another $150 under its matching formula. The account would receive $450 from those two sources for that month, before considering investment gains or losses and any applicable fees.

Because matching formulas differ, read the plan's summary or benefits materials instead of assuming that every employer uses the same match.

Traditional 401(k) vs. Roth 401(k)

Some employers offer both traditional and Roth 401(k) contributions. The biggest difference is generally when the contribution receives its tax treatment.

Feature Traditional 401(k) Roth 401(k)
Contribution tax treatment Contributions generally receive their applicable tax treatment before retirement. Contributions are generally made with after-tax dollars.
Taxes when contributing Generally reduces current taxable income under applicable rules. Does not generally provide the same current-income tax treatment.
Retirement withdrawals Generally taxable according to applicable retirement-account rules. Qualified withdrawals can receive favorable tax treatment when applicable requirements are satisfied.
Who determines availability? Depends on the employer's plan. Depends on whether the employer offers a Roth feature.

There is no universal answer about which tax treatment is better for every person. Income, tax situation, expected future tax rates, retirement timing and other factors can affect the decision.

How Much Should You Contribute to a 401(k)?

The right contribution amount depends on your income, household expenses, employer plan, other savings, debt, emergency reserves and retirement goals.

Instead of starting with an arbitrary percentage, consider these questions:

  • Does your employer offer a matching contribution?
  • What contribution level can your current budget support?
  • Do you have enough cash reserves for foreseeable expenses?
  • Are you carrying high-interest debt that needs attention?
  • Are you saving for other near-term goals?
  • Are you likely to increase your contribution as your income rises?

A practical way to think about your contribution

Start with a contribution amount you can consistently maintain. Understand the employer's matching rules, then review your overall budget before increasing contributions.

A contribution rate that fits your actual cash flow is more useful than a target you repeatedly have to stop or reverse.

How Are 401(k) Contributions Invested?

A 401(k) account is a retirement account, but the money inside the account still needs to be invested according to your plan's available choices.

Employer plans commonly provide a menu of investment options. Depending on the plan, these may include stock funds, bond funds, target-date funds, stable-value options or other investments.

Target-date funds

A target-date fund is designed around an approximate retirement year. Its investment mix typically changes over time according to the fund's strategy.

Stock and bond funds

Some plans allow employees to build their own allocation from stock and bond funds. The appropriate mix depends on factors such as time horizon, risk tolerance and overall financial situation.

Do not ignore fees

Investment expenses and plan administration fees can reduce the amount of money that remains invested. Compare the fees and investment options available in your actual plan rather than focusing only on past performance.

What Does 401(k) Vesting Mean?

Vesting refers to your ownership of employer-provided contributions.

Your own contributions generally belong to you under the applicable retirement-plan rules. Employer contributions can have a separate vesting schedule.

Some plans provide immediate vesting, while others may use a graded or cliff vesting schedule. If you are changing jobs, check the plan documents to understand how much of the employer contribution is vested.

Why this matters:

Two employees could have identical account balances but different amounts of vested employer contributions if their plan rules and employment periods differ.

Common Types of 401(k) Contributions

Traditional employee contributions Contributions made through payroll with the tax treatment provided under traditional 401(k) rules.
Roth employee contributions After-tax employee contributions when the employer's plan offers a Roth 401(k) feature.
Employer matching contributions Employer money contributed according to the plan's matching formula.
Other employer contributions Some employers may make contributions that do not depend on an employee contribution in the same way a match does.

How a 401(k) Affects Your Paycheck

Increasing a 401(k) contribution changes the amount of money available from each paycheck. The exact effect on take-home pay depends on whether the contribution is traditional or Roth and on your overall tax situation.

A traditional contribution can reduce taxable wages for applicable federal income-tax purposes, while payroll taxes and other deductions can have different treatment. Roth contributions generally do not provide the same current federal income-tax treatment.

Because payroll taxes and state rules can differ, use your actual paycheck information when deciding how a contribution change affects your monthly budget.

Ways to Increase Your 401(k) Contributions

You do not necessarily need to make a large contribution change all at once. Gradual increases can make a higher savings rate easier to incorporate into your budget.

  1. Increase after a raise: Directing part of a pay increase toward retirement can reduce the effect on your existing spending plan.
  2. Review your contribution annually: Check your percentage when you receive a new salary or benefits enrollment information.
  3. Automate the decision: Payroll deductions make retirement contributions automatic.
  4. Check your emergency savings: Increasing retirement contributions should be considered alongside your need for accessible cash.
  5. Review your investments: A higher contribution does not fix an investment allocation that does not match your goals or risk tolerance.

What Happens to Your 401(k) When You Change Jobs?

Leaving an employer does not automatically mean your retirement savings disappear. Your available choices depend on the plan, your new employer and applicable retirement account rules.

Depending on the circumstances, possible options can include leaving the money in the former employer's plan, moving it to a new employer plan when permitted, or completing another eligible rollover.

Before moving money, review fees, investment choices, withdrawal rules and the tax consequences of the transaction. A rollover can have important tax and administrative details.

Common 401(k) Mistakes to Avoid

1. Not checking the employer match

If your employer provides matching contributions, understand exactly how the formula works and what conditions apply.

2. Treating the account as automatically invested

Money entering a 401(k) does not necessarily mean you have selected an appropriate investment allocation. Check how your contributions are being invested.

3. Ignoring fees

Even small recurring investment or administrative costs can affect long-term account growth.

4. Forgetting about old retirement accounts

Job changes can leave people with multiple retirement accounts. Keep track of former employer accounts and understand their fees, investment choices and available options.

5. Chasing recent performance

A fund's recent performance does not guarantee future results. Consider the investment's role in your overall allocation rather than choosing solely from the latest return numbers.

6. Increasing contributions without checking cash flow

Retirement savings matter, but your budget also needs to accommodate current bills, emergency savings and other financial priorities.

401(k) Checklist

  • Check whether your employer offers a 401(k).
  • Understand the employer matching formula.
  • Review the traditional and Roth options available.
  • Choose a contribution rate that fits your budget.
  • Confirm how your contributions are invested.
  • Review investment expenses and plan fees.
  • Understand the vesting rules for employer contributions.
  • Keep your beneficiaries up to date.
  • Revisit your contribution rate as your income changes.

401(k) Frequently Asked Questions

What is a 401(k)?

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement plan that allows eligible employees to save through payroll deductions and invest those contributions for retirement.

What is an employer 401(k) match?

An employer match is money an employer contributes to your retirement account according to the plan's rules, often based on how much you contribute.

What is the difference between a traditional and Roth 401(k)?

Traditional contributions generally receive their applicable tax treatment before retirement, while Roth contributions are generally made with after-tax dollars. Withdrawal rules and tax treatment depend on applicable requirements.

How much should I contribute to my 401(k)?

There is no universal percentage. Consider your employer match, income, current expenses, emergency savings, debt, other goals and retirement timeline. Choose a contribution amount you can maintain.

What does vesting mean in a 401(k)?

Vesting determines when employer-provided contributions become fully yours under the plan's rules. Your own contributions generally have different ownership rules.

Can I have both traditional and Roth 401(k) contributions?

Some employer plans allow both types of employee contributions. Availability and applicable limits depend on the plan and current retirement-account rules.

Is a 401(k) the same as a savings account?

No. A 401(k) is a retirement account designed for long-term investing and has specific tax and withdrawal rules. A regular savings account is generally designed for accessible cash and near-term needs.

What happens to my 401(k) if I leave my job?

Depending on your circumstances, you may be able to leave the account in the former employer's plan, move it to a new employer's plan when permitted, or use another eligible rollover option. Review the tax and investment consequences before making a decision.
