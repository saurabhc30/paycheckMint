RETIREMENT INVESTING

Retirement Investing: How to Invest for Retirement

Learn how retirement investing works, how to build a retirement portfolio, choose investments, manage risk, use retirement accounts, and turn long-term savings into retirement income.

Quick answer Retirement investing is the process of investing money during your working years and managing those investments so they can help fund expenses during retirement. A retirement strategy typically considers savings, time horizon, asset allocation, diversification, taxes, investment costs, withdrawal needs, and other sources of retirement income.

What Is Retirement Investing?

Retirement investing means putting money into investments during your working years with the goal of using those assets to help support you later in life.

Retirement can last for decades, which means your money may need to support expenses long after you stop receiving a regular paycheck.

Retirement investing therefore involves two broad stages: building assets before retirement and managing those assets during retirement.

Common retirement investments include stocks, bonds, mutual funds, exchange-traded funds, cash investments, and other securities held inside retirement or taxable investment accounts.

Why Retirement Investing Matters

Saving money is an important part of retirement preparation, but the way savings are invested can affect how much the portfolio grows, how much it fluctuates, and how it can eventually provide income.

Investments can provide long-term growth and income, but investment returns are not guaranteed. The value of investments can also decline.

A retirement plan therefore needs to consider both growth and the need to access money for current expenses.

Think beyond your retirement date.

Retirement investing does not necessarily stop when employment ends. Your portfolio may still need to support spending and purchasing power for many years after retirement.

Start With Your Retirement Income Goal

Before deciding what investments to use, estimate how much money you may need during retirement.

A useful starting point is to compare expected retirement expenses with income from other sources.

Basic retirement income gap

Estimated annual retirement expenses − expected income from other sources = amount your investments may need to help cover

Other income may include Social Security, pension income, part-time employment, rental income, annuities, or other sources depending on your circumstances.

This calculation is only a starting point. Expenses, inflation, taxes, investment returns, and withdrawal needs can change over time.

What Is Asset Allocation?

Asset allocation is the way your portfolio is divided among different asset classes.

A retirement portfolio may contain a combination of:

Stocks

Stocks can provide long-term growth potential but can experience substantial price fluctuations.

Bonds

Bonds can provide interest income and may behave differently from stocks, although bond prices can also fluctuate.

Cash & Short-Term Investments

Cash and short-term investments can help cover near-term spending needs but may provide less long-term growth.

Other Investments

Depending on the investor, a portfolio may also include other diversified investments.

There is no single asset allocation that is appropriate for every investor. Time horizon, spending needs, risk tolerance, other income, and financial circumstances can all affect the decision.

Stocks, Bonds, and Cash in a Retirement Portfolio

Retirement investing often involves balancing three broad objectives: growth, income, and liquidity.

Asset class Potential role Important consideration
Stocks Long-term growth Prices can fluctuate significantly.
Bonds Income and diversification Interest-rate and credit risks apply.
Cash / short-term investments Liquidity and near-term spending Purchasing power can be affected by inflation.

A portfolio's allocation may change as an investor's circumstances, time horizon, and spending needs change.

Retirement Accounts Used for Investing

The account you use can be just as important as the investments held inside it because different accounts can have different tax rules and withdrawal requirements.

401(k) Plans

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement plan. Depending on the plan, contributions may be made on a traditional pre-tax basis, Roth basis, or both.

Learn more in our 401(k) guide .

Traditional IRAs

Traditional IRAs can provide tax-advantaged retirement savings. Tax treatment depends on factors such as contribution eligibility, income, and other retirement coverage.

Roth IRAs

Roth IRAs use after-tax contributions and can provide tax-free qualified withdrawals under applicable rules.

Compare the two account structures in our Roth IRA vs. Traditional IRA guide .

Taxable Investment Accounts

A taxable brokerage account does not have the same retirement-account tax structure, but it can provide flexibility for investors who need money outside retirement accounts.

Why Diversification Matters for Retirement

Diversification means spreading investments across different securities, companies, sectors, geographic markets, or asset classes instead of relying heavily on one investment.

Diversification cannot eliminate investment losses, but it can reduce the effect that one investment or market segment has on the overall portfolio.

Mutual funds and ETFs can make diversification easier by holding many securities within one investment. However, diversification depends on what the fund actually owns.

Retirement Investing Risks to Understand

Retirement investing involves several types of risk. Understanding them can help you evaluate whether a particular investment approach fits your circumstances.

Market Risk

Investment prices can decline, sometimes sharply, reducing portfolio value.

Inflation Risk

Rising prices can reduce the purchasing power of retirement savings and income.

Interest-Rate Risk

Changes in interest rates can affect bond prices and other investments.

Longevity Risk

You may live longer than expected, requiring your assets to support you for additional years.

Sequence-of-Returns Risk

Poor investment returns early in retirement can have a larger effect when withdrawals are occurring.

Tax Risk

Taxes can affect how much of your investment income and withdrawals you actually have available to spend.

Retirement Investing Before You Retire

During the accumulation phase, investors generally have time to contribute regularly and allow potential investment growth to compound.

  1. Set a retirement target. Estimate when you want to retire and what your expected spending could look like.
  2. Review your savings rate. Look at how much of your income is going toward retirement accounts and other investments.
  3. Understand available tax-advantaged accounts. Review the retirement accounts available through your employer and individually.
  4. Build a diversified portfolio. Consider how stocks, bonds, cash, and other investments fit your time horizon and risk tolerance.
  5. Review your investments periodically. Changes in income, goals, markets, and account balances can affect whether your portfolio remains aligned with your plan.

Retirement Investing as You Get Closer to Retirement

As retirement approaches, the purpose of your portfolio may begin to change. You may need to think more carefully about when and how you will use your investments.

Important questions include:

  • How much income will I need from my portfolio?
  • What other retirement income will I receive?
  • How much portfolio volatility can I tolerate?
  • Which accounts will I use first?
  • How will taxes affect my withdrawals?
  • How much cash or short-term money should be available for near-term expenses?
  • How will the portfolio support a potentially long retirement?

Turning Retirement Savings Into Income

Retirement investing eventually changes from primarily accumulating assets to using those assets.

Potential retirement income sources include:

  • Social Security
  • Pension income
  • 401(k) and other workplace retirement plans
  • Traditional and Roth IRAs
  • Taxable investment accounts
  • Interest and dividends
  • Other personal income sources

Retirement income planning involves considering how these sources work together and how much must come from investment assets.

How Retirement Withdrawals Work

A withdrawal strategy determines how you access retirement savings after you stop working.

Your strategy can involve decisions about:

  • Which accounts to withdraw from
  • How much to withdraw
  • How frequently to withdraw
  • How taxes affect each withdrawal
  • Required minimum distributions when applicable
  • How much should remain invested for future expenses
There is no universal withdrawal percentage.

A withdrawal strategy should account for factors such as portfolio size, spending, other income, age, investment allocation, taxes, market conditions, and expected longevity.

What Is Portfolio Rebalancing?

Over time, investment performance can change the percentage of your portfolio held in each asset class.

For example, if stocks rise faster than bonds, stocks may become a larger percentage of the portfolio than originally intended.

Rebalancing means adjusting the portfolio back toward a chosen asset allocation.

Rebalancing can also have tax consequences in taxable accounts, so investors should consider account type and transaction costs when evaluating changes.

Pay Attention to Investment Fees

Investment costs reduce the amount of money that remains invested. Over a long retirement-investing period, even relatively small recurring costs can affect portfolio growth.

When evaluating an investment, review costs such as:

  • Expense ratios
  • Trading costs
  • Account fees
  • Advisory fees
  • Sales charges, where applicable
  • Other transaction or service fees

Compare fees alongside an investment's strategy, risks, holdings, services, and potential tax consequences rather than evaluating cost by itself.

Common Retirement Investing Mistakes

  • Ignoring inflation: Future expenses may be higher than today's expenses.
  • Taking more risk than intended: Your portfolio should reflect your ability and willingness to tolerate losses.
  • Taking too little risk for too long: An overly conservative portfolio may have difficulty keeping pace with long-term inflation and spending needs.
  • Lack of diversification: Concentrating too much money in one investment or sector can increase portfolio-specific risk.
  • Ignoring taxes: The amount available to spend can differ depending on the account and withdrawal.
  • Failing to plan withdrawals: Accumulating a large balance does not automatically create a sustainable retirement income strategy.
  • Forgetting about longevity: Retirement savings may need to last much longer than originally expected.
  • Reacting emotionally to market declines: Major portfolio changes during market stress can affect a long-term retirement plan.

Retirement Investing Checklist

  1. Estimate your expected retirement spending.
  2. List expected income sources outside your portfolio.
  3. Estimate the amount your investments may need to provide.
  4. Review your retirement account options.
  5. Choose an asset allocation that reflects your circumstances.
  6. Diversify across appropriate investments.
  7. Review investment costs.
  8. Consider taxes and account types.
  9. Create a withdrawal strategy before retirement.
  10. Review and update your plan as circumstances change.

Retirement Investing FAQ

What is retirement investing?

Retirement investing is the process of investing money during your working years and managing those investments so they can help fund expenses during retirement.

How should I invest for retirement?

A retirement investment approach generally considers your time horizon, income needs, risk tolerance, diversification, taxes, investment costs, and available retirement accounts.

Should a retirement portfolio include stocks and bonds?

A retirement portfolio may include stocks, bonds, and cash investments. The appropriate mix depends on factors such as time horizon, spending needs, risk tolerance, and other sources of retirement income.

Can I invest after I retire?

Yes. Retirement does not necessarily mean stopping investment activity. Retirees may continue investing while also withdrawing money to pay expenses.

What is asset allocation in retirement?

Asset allocation is how a portfolio is divided among asset classes such as stocks, bonds, and cash or short-term investments.

How do I turn retirement savings into income?

Retirement income can come from Social Security, pensions, investment income, and withdrawals from retirement and taxable accounts. The appropriate approach depends on individual circumstances.

What risks should retirees consider when investing?

Important risks include market volatility, inflation, interest-rate changes, longevity, sequence-of-returns risk, taxes, and withdrawing too much or too little.

Is retirement investing different from investing when I am younger?

The goals and time horizon can change as retirement approaches. Investors often pay more attention to withdrawal needs, portfolio volatility, income sources, taxes, and the possibility of needing assets for several decades.
