What does compound interest mean?

Compound interest is growth earned on an amount that already includes previous growth. When money remains invested, returns can potentially generate additional returns over subsequent periods.

The calculator above combines a starting investment, monthly contributions, an assumed annual return, and a time period to estimate a possible future value.

How compound interest works

With simple interest, growth is calculated only on the original principal. With compounding, previously earned growth becomes part of the balance that can generate additional growth.

The basic compound-interest formula for a lump-sum investment is:

A = P × (1 + r / n)nt
Basic compound-interest formula
A = ending value
P = starting principal
r = annual rate expressed as a decimal
n = number of compounding periods per year
t = number of years

Regular contributions add another variable. Each contribution has its own amount of time to potentially grow, which means contributions made earlier generally have more compounding periods than contributions made later.

Example: investing $5,000 plus $300 a month

Suppose you start with $5,000, contribute $300 at the end of every month, use a 7% annual return assumption, and leave the money invested for 20 years.

The calculator can estimate the ending balance and show how much came from your starting investment, how much you contributed over time, and how much is attributed to the assumed growth rate.

The 7% figure in this example is only an assumption. It should not be interpreted as a guaranteed investment return or a prediction of future market performance.

Why time matters when money compounds

Compounding depends on repeated periods of growth. Giving an investment more time creates more opportunities for previous growth and new contributions to participate in subsequent periods.

This is one reason an investor may want to compare several time horizons rather than looking at only one target date. Try the calculator with 10, 20, 30, and 40 years while keeping the other assumptions unchanged.

Starting later does not make saving pointless. It simply means there are fewer periods in which the money can potentially compound, so the contribution and time assumptions become especially important to the estimate.

Compound interest vs. simple interest

The main difference is the amount on which future growth is calculated. Compound growth includes accumulated growth in the balance, while simple interest is calculated from the original principal.

Feature Simple interest Compound interest
Growth base Original principal Principal plus accumulated growth
Previous growth Not added to the calculation base Can become part of the balance
Effect of time Growth generally increases linearly Growth can accelerate as the balance increases
Common concept Interest on principal Interest or returns on accumulated value

How compounding frequency affects growth

Compounding frequency describes how often growth is added to the balance. Common frequencies include annual, semi-annual, quarterly, monthly, and daily.

Annual

Growth is compounded once per year.

Semi-annual

Growth is compounded twice per year.

Quarterly

Growth is compounded four times per year.

Monthly or daily

Growth is modeled more frequently during the year.

More frequent compounding can produce a somewhat different result when the nominal annual rate is held constant. However, the effect should be considered alongside the contribution amount, investment period, fees, taxes, and actual investment performance.

How regular contributions affect compound growth

Regular contributions can increase the amount of money available to compound. They also mean that your total invested amount grows over time instead of remaining fixed.

For example, increasing a monthly contribution changes both the amount you personally add and the amount that can potentially experience future growth.

You can test this directly by running the calculator twice: once with your current monthly contribution and again with a higher or lower amount. Keep the other assumptions the same so you can isolate the effect of the contribution.

What this calculator does not include

This is a simplified planning calculator. Real investment outcomes can differ because many factors are not modeled.

  • Investment taxes
  • Account fees and expense ratios
  • Inflation
  • Changing investment returns
  • Withdrawals
  • Employer contributions or matches
  • Account-specific contribution limits
  • Changes to your monthly contribution

Because of these exclusions, use the result as an illustration rather than a precise forecast of what an investment account will actually be worth.

Practical ways to use the calculator

  1. Compare time horizons. Try different investment periods to see how the assumed growth changes.
  2. Test contribution amounts. Compare several monthly contribution levels based on amounts that fit your budget.
  3. Test different return assumptions. Use more than one assumption rather than relying on a single rate.
  4. Look at the breakdown. Separate your contributions from the estimated growth instead of focusing only on the final number.
  5. Revisit the estimate. Your income, savings rate, time horizon, and goals can change over time.

Compound interest calculator FAQ

What is compound interest?

Compound interest is growth calculated on an amount that includes previous accumulated growth. Over multiple periods, the growth itself can become part of the balance used for future growth.

How does compound interest work with monthly contributions?

Each monthly contribution becomes part of the balance and can potentially grow during the remaining investment period. Earlier contributions have more time to compound than later contributions.

Is a 7% return guaranteed?

No. A 7% rate in a calculator is simply an assumption. Investment returns are uncertain and actual results can be higher or lower.

Does daily compounding always make a big difference?

More frequent compounding can change the result when other assumptions remain constant, but the difference may be relatively small. The contribution amount and length of time invested can also have substantial effects on an estimate.

How long does it take for money to compound?

There is no single time period that applies to everyone. The result depends on the starting amount, contribution rate, return assumption, compounding method, and investment period.

Should I account for inflation?

For longer-term planning, considering inflation can be useful because a future dollar may buy less than a dollar does today. This calculator shows a nominal growth estimate and does not subtract inflation.

Does the calculator include taxes and fees?

No. Taxes, investment fees, expense ratios, withdrawals, inflation, and account-specific rules are not included in the basic calculation.
