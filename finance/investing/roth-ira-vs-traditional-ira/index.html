Roth IRA vs. Traditional IRA

Roth IRAs and Traditional IRAs can both be used to save and invest for retirement, but they provide their tax benefits at different times. Understanding contributions, deductions, income limits, withdrawals, and required minimum distributions can help you compare the two account types.

Quick answer: A Traditional IRA generally provides its potential tax benefit when you contribute, because eligible contributions may be deductible. A Roth IRA generally provides its main tax benefit later, because qualified withdrawals can be tax-free. The right account depends on factors such as your income, current tax situation, expected future tax situation, retirement goals, and eligibility.

Roth IRA vs. Traditional IRA: Key differences

The biggest difference between the two accounts is how and when taxes apply. A Traditional IRA can offer a deduction for eligible contributions, while a Roth IRA does not provide a deduction for contributions. Instead, Roth contributions are made with money that has already been taxed, and qualified withdrawals can generally be tax-free.

Feature Roth IRA Traditional IRA
Contributions Made with after-tax money May be deductible if you qualify
Tax deduction Contributions are not deductible Contributions may be deductible
Investment growth Potentially tax-free when qualified Generally tax-deferred
Qualified retirement withdrawals Generally tax-free Generally taxable as ordinary income
Roth income eligibility Income limits apply to direct contributions No income limit for making a contribution, but deduction rules can be limited
Lifetime RMDs for original owner Generally no Generally yes
Best-known tax advantage Potential tax-free qualified withdrawals Potential deduction today and tax-deferred growth

These are general rules. Specific eligibility, deduction, withdrawal, and tax treatment can depend on your circumstances and the applicable IRS rules.

How does a Roth IRA work?

A Roth IRA is an individual retirement account funded with after-tax money. You generally do not deduct your Roth IRA contribution from your taxable income.

Once money is inside the account, you can invest it in the investments available through your IRA provider. Potential investment growth can accumulate without current federal income tax, and qualified Roth IRA distributions can generally be made tax-free.

Pay taxes before contributing

Roth IRA contributions are made with money that has already been included in taxable income.

Potential tax-free withdrawals

Qualified withdrawals can generally be made without federal income tax, subject to applicable requirements.

No lifetime RMDs for the owner

Unlike a Traditional IRA, the original owner of a Roth IRA generally does not have lifetime required minimum distributions.

Income limits apply

Direct Roth IRA contributions can be reduced or eliminated at higher modified adjusted gross income levels.

How does a Traditional IRA work?

A Traditional IRA is a retirement account in which contributions may be tax-deductible depending on your income, filing status, and workplace retirement-plan coverage.

Investments inside the account generally grow tax-deferred. When you take taxable distributions, amounts that were deductible generally become taxable as ordinary income.

Importantly, being allowed to contribute to a Traditional IRA is not the same as being entitled to deduct the contribution. The IRS has separate rules for contribution eligibility and deduction eligibility.

2026 IRA contribution limits

The IRS limits the total amount you can contribute to your Traditional and Roth IRAs each year. The limit applies across the two account types rather than giving you a separate full limit for each account.

$7,500 2026 standard IRA contribution limit

The combined annual limit for Traditional and Roth IRAs is $7,500 for people under age 50, subject to taxable compensation rules.

$8,600 2026 limit for age 50 and older

People age 50 or older can generally make an additional $1,100 catch-up contribution, bringing the 2026 limit to $8,600.

For example, if you contribute $4,000 to a Roth IRA during 2026, you generally cannot contribute another $7,500 to a Traditional IRA. Your remaining combined IRA contribution capacity would generally be $3,500, assuming you otherwise qualify.

Important: The IRA contribution limit is different from the contribution limit for a 401(k). If you have a workplace 401(k), that account has its own annual limits.

Roth IRA income limits vs. Traditional IRA rules

Income is one of the most important differences between the two account types.

Roth IRA income limits

Your ability to make a direct Roth IRA contribution can depend on your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) and tax filing status. For 2026, the Roth IRA phaseout ranges include:

Filing status 2026 MAGI range General effect
Single / Head of household Below $153,000 Full contribution generally permitted
Single / Head of household $153,000 to below $168,000 Contribution is reduced
Single / Head of household $168,000 or more Direct Roth contribution is generally not permitted
Married filing jointly Below $242,000 Full contribution generally permitted
Married filing jointly $242,000 to below $252,000 Contribution is reduced
Married filing jointly $252,000 or more Direct Roth contribution is generally not permitted

Married filing separately has different rules, including a much lower phaseout range when the taxpayer lived with their spouse during the year.

Traditional IRA income rules

There is generally no income limit preventing you from making a Traditional IRA contribution if you have sufficient taxable compensation. However, your ability to deduct that contribution can be limited by income and workplace retirement-plan coverage.

For 2026, for example, if you are covered by a retirement plan at work, the Traditional IRA deduction phaseout for a single filer generally runs from more than $81,000 to less than $91,000 of modified AGI. For married couples filing jointly, the range is generally more than $129,000 to less than $149,000 when the contributor is covered by a workplace plan.

IRS deduction rules are more detailed for married taxpayers and situations involving a spouse covered by a workplace plan. Check the current IRS rules before determining your deduction.

Roth IRA vs. Traditional IRA withdrawals

Withdrawal taxation is another major difference.

Roth IRA withdrawals

Roth IRA contributions are made after tax. Under IRS rules, Roth IRA withdrawals have ordering rules that generally treat regular contributions as coming out before earnings.

Qualified distributions of Roth earnings can generally be tax-free when applicable requirements are satisfied, including the applicable five-year rule and qualifying conditions such as reaching age 59½.

Traditional IRA withdrawals

Traditional IRA withdrawals are generally included in taxable income to the extent they represent taxable amounts.

Withdrawals before age 59½ can also be subject to an additional 10% tax unless an exception applies.

Don't confuse contribution access with qualified earnings withdrawals. Roth IRA rules can allow contributions to be withdrawn under different rules from investment earnings. A withdrawal that is permitted does not automatically mean every dollar withdrawn is tax-free.

Required minimum distributions: Roth vs. Traditional IRA

Required minimum distributions, or RMDs, are minimum amounts that generally must be withdrawn from certain retirement accounts once applicable rules require them.

Traditional IRA

Traditional IRA owners generally must begin taking RMDs under the applicable federal rules. The current general starting age for many individuals is 73, although the exact rules can depend on circumstances and changes in tax law.

Roth IRA

The original owner of a Roth IRA generally does not have to take lifetime RMDs. This allows the account to remain invested during the owner's lifetime without a mandatory lifetime distribution schedule.

Inherited IRAs have separate distribution rules. Do not assume that the lifetime RMD treatment of an original Roth IRA owner applies automatically to beneficiaries.

Tax treatment: when do you pay taxes?

Stage Roth IRA Traditional IRA
When contributing Contribute after-tax money Contribution may be deductible if eligible
While invested Potential investment growth is generally not currently taxed Investment growth is generally tax-deferred
Qualified retirement withdrawal Generally tax-free Generally taxable as ordinary income

This is why people sometimes describe the decision as paying taxes now versus potentially paying taxes later. That description is useful, but it is an oversimplification because Traditional IRA deductibility and Roth IRA eligibility depend on specific IRS rules.

Roth IRA vs. Traditional IRA: investment choices

A Roth IRA and Traditional IRA are account types, not individual investments. The investments available inside an IRA depend on the financial institution and account.

Depending on the provider, an IRA may allow investments such as:

  • Stocks
  • Bonds
  • Exchange-traded funds (ETFs)
  • Mutual funds
  • Certificates of deposit or other eligible products
  • Target-date funds

The tax type of the account does not automatically determine the investment strategy. You still need to consider diversification, time horizon, risk, fees, and your retirement goals.

How to compare a Roth IRA and Traditional IRA

Instead of assuming one account is universally better, compare the features against your own circumstances.

1. Consider your current tax situation

A Traditional IRA can provide a deduction if you qualify. That can make the current-year tax treatment an important consideration.

2. Consider your expected retirement tax situation

Roth and Traditional IRAs shift the tax benefit to different points in time. Your expectations about future taxable income and tax rates can therefore be relevant.

3. Check Roth IRA income eligibility

Higher-income taxpayers may not be eligible to make a direct Roth IRA contribution or may be limited to a reduced contribution.

4. Check whether a Traditional IRA contribution is deductible

Being eligible to contribute does not necessarily mean the full contribution is deductible.

5. Consider withdrawal flexibility

Roth and Traditional IRAs have different withdrawal and tax rules. If access to retirement money before traditional retirement age is an important concern, understand those rules before contributing.

6. Consider required distributions

Traditional IRA owners generally face lifetime RMD requirements, while original Roth IRA owners generally do not.

Can you have both a Roth IRA and a Traditional IRA?

Yes. You can generally have both account types and contribute to both if you satisfy the applicable rules.

However, having two accounts does not give you two separate annual contribution limits. The annual IRA contribution limit applies to your combined contributions to Traditional and Roth IRAs.

For example, a person under age 50 who contributes $3,000 to a Traditional IRA in 2026 could generally have up to $4,500 of remaining IRA contribution capacity for a Roth IRA, assuming the person otherwise qualifies and has sufficient taxable compensation.

Tax diversification: Some retirement savers use a combination of account types to create a mix of potentially taxable and tax-free retirement income sources. Whether that approach makes sense depends on the person's circumstances.

Roth IRA vs. Traditional IRA example

Imagine two workers who each have $6,000 available to save for retirement.

Roth IRA example

The worker contributes after-tax money to a Roth IRA. The contribution does not provide a current federal income tax deduction. If the account satisfies the applicable requirements for qualified distributions, withdrawals in retirement can generally be tax-free.

Traditional IRA example

The worker contributes to a Traditional IRA. If the worker qualifies for a deduction, the contribution may reduce taxable income for the applicable year. Taxable withdrawals in retirement are generally taxed as ordinary income.

The investment performance itself is not determined by whether the account is Roth or Traditional. The important difference is the tax treatment surrounding the account.

Common IRA mistakes to avoid

1. Treating the Roth and Traditional limits as separate

The annual contribution limit applies to your combined Traditional and Roth IRA contributions.

2. Assuming every Traditional IRA contribution is deductible

Deductibility can be restricted by income, filing status, and workplace retirement-plan coverage.

3. Ignoring Roth income limits

A direct Roth contribution can be reduced or eliminated when MAGI reaches the applicable IRS phaseout levels.

4. Assuming every Roth withdrawal is tax-free

Roth contributions and earnings are subject to different rules. Qualified distributions have specific requirements.

5. Forgetting about RMDs

Traditional IRA owners generally need to account for required minimum distributions under federal rules.

6. Contributing without investing the money

Depositing money into an IRA does not automatically mean it has been invested. Check your account after making a contribution to understand where the money is held.

7. Ignoring fees and investment choices

Compare account costs, available investments, trading features, customer support, and other provider-specific features when selecting an IRA provider.

Roth IRA vs. Traditional IRA checklist

  1. Estimate your taxable income and modified adjusted gross income.
  2. Identify your tax filing status.
  3. Check the current Roth IRA income limits.
  4. Determine whether you or your spouse is covered by a workplace retirement plan.
  5. Check whether a Traditional IRA contribution would be deductible.
  6. Review the current annual IRA contribution limit.
  7. Consider how current and future taxes may affect your retirement income.
  8. Review withdrawal and RMD rules.
  9. Compare investment options and fees at potential IRA providers.
  10. Keep records of contributions and tax documents.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between a Roth IRA and a Traditional IRA?

A Traditional IRA can provide a tax deduction for eligible contributions, while a Roth IRA does not. Roth contributions are made after tax and qualified withdrawals can generally be tax-free. Traditional IRA withdrawals are generally taxable to the extent they represent taxable amounts.

Is a Roth IRA better than a Traditional IRA?

Neither account is universally better. The relevant differences include current tax treatment, expected future tax situation, income eligibility, deduction eligibility, withdrawal rules, and RMD requirements.

Can I contribute to both a Roth IRA and Traditional IRA?

Yes, if you are eligible. However, the annual IRA contribution limit applies to the combined contributions made to both account types.

What is the IRA contribution limit for 2026?

For 2026, the combined contribution limit for Traditional and Roth IRAs is $7,500 for people under age 50 and $8,600 for people age 50 or older, subject to taxable compensation and applicable IRS rules.

Can high-income earners contribute to a Traditional IRA?

There is generally no income limit for making a Traditional IRA contribution if you otherwise qualify. However, income can affect whether the contribution is deductible.

Can high-income earners contribute directly to a Roth IRA?

Direct Roth IRA contributions are subject to income phaseout rules. At sufficiently high MAGI levels, a direct Roth contribution is not permitted.

Are Roth IRA contributions tax deductible?

No. Roth IRA contributions are made with after-tax money and are not deductible from federal taxable income.

Are Traditional IRA contributions tax deductible?

They may be. The deduction depends on factors including income, filing status, and whether you or your spouse is covered by a workplace retirement plan.

Does a Roth IRA have required minimum distributions?

The original owner of a Roth IRA generally does not have lifetime required minimum distributions.

When can I withdraw money from a Roth IRA tax-free?

Qualified Roth IRA distributions generally require the applicable five-year rule and a qualifying condition such as reaching age 59½. Roth withdrawal rules contain additional provisions and exceptions, so individual withdrawals should be evaluated carefully.

Can I roll a 401(k) into a Roth IRA?

A rollover or conversion from a workplace retirement plan to a Roth IRA can have tax consequences and is different from simply contributing to a Roth IRA. The treatment depends on the type of plan funds and transaction.
