Roth IRA vs Traditional IRA

Roth and Traditional IRAs are both tax-advantaged retirement accounts, but they generally provide their tax benefits at different points in the process. Understanding contributions, deductions, withdrawals, eligibility, and tax timing can help you compare the two accounts in context.

At a glance

The biggest difference is usually tax timing

Roth IRA

  • Contributions are generally after-tax.
  • Contributions are generally not deductible.
  • Qualified withdrawals are generally tax-free.

Traditional IRA

  • Contributions may be deductible.
  • Tax treatment can occur when money is withdrawn.
  • Required distribution rules generally apply later in life.

Eligibility, deduction rules, contribution limits, and withdrawal rules depend on applicable tax law and your circumstances.

Quick answer

A Traditional IRA generally provides its potential tax benefit upfront through deductible contributions, subject to eligibility and applicable rules, while a Roth IRA generally uses after-tax contributions in exchange for tax-free qualified withdrawals. The account that fits a particular person depends on factors such as current and expected future taxes, eligibility, income, retirement goals, and how the money may eventually be withdrawn.

What is an IRA?

An individual retirement arrangement, commonly called an IRA, is a tax-advantaged account designed to help individuals save and invest for retirement.

An IRA is an account rather than a single investment. Depending on the provider, money inside an IRA may be invested in assets such as mutual funds, exchange-traded funds, stocks, bonds, or other available investments.

Roth and Traditional IRAs share many characteristics, including annual contribution limits and rules governing eligibility and distributions. Their biggest structural difference is generally the timing of the tax benefit.

The key difference: when you pay taxes

One of the easiest ways to understand the two accounts is to look at when the tax benefit generally occurs.

Roth IRA

You generally contribute after-tax money. Qualified withdrawals in retirement are generally tax-free.

→

Traditional IRA

A qualifying contribution may provide a tax deduction now, while distributions are generally taxable later.

Tax-free does not mean tax-free at every stage

Roth IRA contributions are generally made with after-tax money. The tax advantage generally applies to qualified distributions rather than making the original contribution deductible.

How a Roth IRA works

A Roth IRA is generally funded with money that has already been included in taxable income. Unlike a Traditional IRA contribution, a Roth IRA contribution is generally not deductible.

Investments inside the account can grow without current federal income tax on each transaction. Qualified distributions can generally be taken tax-free under applicable rules.

Important Roth IRA characteristics

  • Contributions are generally made with after-tax dollars.
  • Contributions are generally not deductible.
  • Qualified distributions are generally tax-free.
  • Direct contribution eligibility can be limited by income.
  • Roth IRAs generally do not require minimum distributions for the original owner under current federal rules.

How a Traditional IRA works

A Traditional IRA generally allows you to contribute money that may qualify for a tax deduction, depending on your income, filing status, workplace retirement plan coverage, and applicable tax rules.

Investments can grow tax-deferred inside the account. When money is distributed, Traditional IRA withdrawals are generally included in taxable income to the extent applicable.

Important Traditional IRA characteristics

  • A contribution may be deductible depending on the circumstances.
  • Investment growth is generally tax-deferred while held inside the account.
  • Withdrawals are generally taxable to the extent applicable.
  • Required minimum distribution rules generally apply.
  • Early withdrawals can involve taxes and potentially an additional tax unless an exception applies.

Why Traditional IRA deductibility matters

A Traditional IRA contribution and a deductible Traditional IRA contribution are not necessarily the same thing.

Depending on your circumstances, you may be able to contribute to a Traditional IRA but receive only a partial deduction or no deduction. Workplace retirement-plan coverage and income can affect the deduction rules.

This distinction is important when comparing the accounts because the tax benefit of a Traditional IRA can be different for two people who contribute the same amount.

Contribution eligibility and deduction eligibility are separate questions

Before assuming that a Traditional IRA contribution will reduce taxable income, check the rules that apply to your filing status, income, and retirement-plan coverage.

Who can contribute to an IRA?

IRA contribution rules depend on several factors, including earned income, age-related rules, income limits for certain contributions, and applicable tax-year rules.

Roth IRA eligibility is particularly important to check because direct Roth contributions can be restricted at higher income levels.

Traditional IRA contributions have different rules. Even when someone can contribute to a Traditional IRA, whether that contribution is deductible can depend on income and workplace-plan coverage.

Because contribution limits and income thresholds can change by tax year, use the rules for the specific year in which you are making the contribution.

What if you already have a 401(k)?

Having an employer-sponsored retirement plan does not automatically mean that you cannot contribute to an IRA.

However, a workplace plan can affect whether a Traditional IRA contribution is deductible. Income can also affect direct Roth IRA eligibility.

This is one reason it is useful to separate three questions:

  1. Can you contribute to the account?
  2. Will the contribution receive a tax deduction?
  3. How will withdrawals be treated later?

The answers can differ depending on the account and your circumstances.

How Roth and Traditional IRA withdrawals differ

Withdrawal rules are another major difference between the accounts.

Roth IRA withdrawals

Roth IRA distributions have specific rules that determine whether they are qualified. Qualified distributions are generally tax-free. Roth IRA contribution basis can also have different treatment from investment earnings.

Traditional IRA withdrawals

Traditional IRA distributions are generally taxable as ordinary income to the extent applicable. Early distributions can also potentially be subject to an additional tax unless an exception applies.

Retirement accounts are designed for long-term use

Do not assume that money can be withdrawn from either IRA at any time without tax consequences. The rules depend on the type of distribution, your age, account history, and applicable exceptions.

Required minimum distributions

Required minimum distributions, commonly called RMDs, are generally required from Traditional IRAs beginning at an applicable age under federal tax rules.

Roth IRAs generally do not require RMDs for the original owner under current federal rules.

This difference can matter for people who want more control over the timing of withdrawals later in retirement. Beneficiary rules can be different, however, so inherited retirement accounts require separate consideration.

Simple examples of the tax trade-off

Consider two hypothetical investors who each contribute the same amount to a retirement account. The accounts can provide different tax benefits because the tax treatment occurs at different times.

R

Roth example

An investor pays applicable taxes on income before contributing to a Roth IRA. If the eventual distribution meets the requirements for qualified treatment, the withdrawal is generally not subject to federal income tax.

T

Traditional example

An investor makes a Traditional IRA contribution that qualifies for a deduction. The investor receives the tax benefit earlier, while applicable taxes are generally paid when taxable distributions are taken later.

Factors to consider when comparing the two

There is no single IRA type that fits every taxpayer. The following questions can help you understand the trade-offs.

1

What is your tax situation today?

If a Traditional IRA contribution is deductible, the current tax deduction can be relevant to the comparison.

2

What might your tax situation look like later?

The relative value of paying taxes now versus later depends partly on your future circumstances, which cannot be known with certainty.

3

Are you eligible?

Income and other rules can affect Roth contribution eligibility and Traditional IRA deductibility.

4

How important is withdrawal flexibility?

Roth and Traditional IRAs have different rules for contributions, earnings, distributions, and required withdrawals.

5

Do you have an employer retirement plan?

A 401(k) or similar workplace plan can affect Traditional IRA deduction rules and your overall retirement strategy.

6

Are you prioritizing retirement savings consistently?

The account choice matters, but regularly saving and investing for retirement is also an important part of the bigger picture.

Situations to examine when comparing Roth and Traditional IRAs

Rather than treating one account as universally preferable, consider which tax characteristics fit the situation you are evaluating.

1

Current tax deduction is important

If you qualify for a Traditional IRA deduction, reducing taxable income in the contribution year may be an important part of the comparison.

2

Future tax-free qualified withdrawals matter

A Roth IRA's treatment of qualified distributions can be relevant when comparing how taxes may be handled in retirement.

3

Income affects eligibility

If your income is near an applicable threshold, eligibility and deduction rules deserve particular attention.

4

You already have several retirement accounts

Look at the tax treatment of your entire retirement strategy rather than evaluating one account in isolation.

IRA contribution limits

Roth and Traditional IRAs share an annual IRA contribution limit. Having both accounts does not generally give you a separate full contribution limit for each one.

For example, if the applicable annual limit allows you to contribute a certain maximum amount across your IRAs, splitting that contribution between a Roth IRA and a Traditional IRA does not generally double the total.

Always check the tax year

IRA contribution limits and income thresholds can change. Use the rules for the specific tax year rather than relying on an old contribution limit.

Can you have both a Roth IRA and Traditional IRA?

Yes. An individual can generally have both types of IRA. The important point is that the annual contribution limit generally applies across your IRAs rather than giving you two completely separate annual limits.

Some investors may use both account types as part of a broader retirement strategy, but the appropriate approach depends on tax circumstances, eligibility, goals, and other retirement accounts.

Roth vs Traditional IRA checklist

Before opening or contributing to an IRA, consider these questions:

✓ What is my current federal tax situation?
✓ Could I deduct a Traditional IRA contribution?
✓ Am I eligible to make a direct Roth IRA contribution?
✓ Do I already participate in a 401(k) or another workplace retirement plan?
✓ How important is the tax treatment of future withdrawals?
✓ Am I saving consistently enough for retirement?
✓ Have I checked the contribution and income rules for the current tax year?

Common IRA mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming Traditional IRA contributions are always deductible. Deductibility can depend on your circumstances.
  • Assuming every Roth withdrawal is automatically tax-free. Qualified-distribution rules matter.
  • Using an outdated contribution limit. Limits can change by tax year.
  • Ignoring income eligibility. Income can affect Roth eligibility and Traditional IRA deductions.
  • Choosing an account without considering your overall retirement plan. Employer plans, taxable investments, and other retirement accounts can affect the bigger picture.
  • Forgetting that an IRA is an account, not the investment itself. After contributing, you generally need to select investments available through your provider.

IRA vs 401(k): how are they different?

A Roth or Traditional IRA is an individual retirement account, while a 401(k) is generally an employer- sponsored retirement plan. They have different contribution limits, investment menus, employer contribution features, and tax rules.

Feature IRA 401(k)
Who establishes it? Individual Employer-sponsored plan
Investment choices Depend on IRA provider Depend on the employer's plan
Employer match Generally no employer match May offer employer contributions
Contribution rules Subject to IRA rules and limits Subject to workplace-plan rules and limits

Because the accounts serve different structures, an IRA does not necessarily replace a workplace retirement plan. Your circumstances determine how the accounts can work together.

Roth IRA vs Traditional IRA FAQs

What is the main difference between a Roth IRA and Traditional IRA?

The main difference is generally when taxes are paid. Traditional IRA contributions may be deductible depending on the circumstances, while Roth contributions are generally made with after-tax money and qualified Roth withdrawals are generally tax-free.

Are Roth IRA contributions tax deductible?

Generally, no. Roth IRA contributions are generally made with after-tax money and are not deductible on your federal income tax return.

Are Traditional IRA contributions tax deductible?

They may be. Deductibility can depend on factors including income, filing status, workplace retirement-plan coverage, and applicable tax rules.

Are Roth IRA withdrawals tax-free?

Qualified Roth IRA distributions are generally tax-free under federal rules. The requirements for qualified treatment still apply.

Are Traditional IRA withdrawals taxable?

Traditional IRA distributions are generally taxable as ordinary income to the extent applicable. The exact treatment can vary depending on the taxpayer's circumstances.

Can I have both a Roth IRA and Traditional IRA?

Yes, you can generally have both. However, annual IRA contribution limits generally apply across your Roth and Traditional IRAs rather than giving you a separate full limit for each.

Which is better: Roth IRA or Traditional IRA?

There is no universal answer. The relevant comparison depends on your current tax situation, expected future circumstances, eligibility, deduction rules, retirement goals, and withdrawal preferences.

Can I contribute to an IRA if I have a 401(k)?

Generally, yes. However, having a workplace plan can affect whether a Traditional IRA contribution is deductible, and income can affect direct Roth IRA eligibility.

Does a Roth IRA grow tax-free?

Investments inside a Roth IRA can grow without current federal income tax on each investment transaction, and qualified distributions are generally tax-free. The account's investments can still gain or lose value.

Does a Traditional IRA grow tax-free?

A Traditional IRA generally provides tax-deferred treatment while assets remain in the account. Tax is generally addressed when taxable distributions are taken.
