Credit & Debt Guide

How to Build Credit

Building credit takes time, but the basic process is straightforward: establish accounts that are appropriate for you, pay them on time, manage balances carefully, and give your credit history time to develop.

Start here

Focus on habits, not a magic number

A strong credit profile is generally built through consistent financial behavior over time.

  • ✓ Pay every bill on time
  • ✓ Keep revolving balances manageable
  • ✓ Apply for credit selectively
  • ✓ Review your credit reports
Quick answer

To build credit, establish a credit history that you can manage responsibly, make payments on time, keep credit-card balances under control, avoid unnecessary applications for new credit, and monitor your credit reports for errors. You do not need to carry a balance and pay interest simply to build credit.

What Does It Mean to Build Credit?

Building credit means developing a record of responsible borrowing and repayment that can be reflected in your credit reports and used by credit-scoring models.

When you have little or no credit history, lenders may have less information available to evaluate your credit behavior. Over time, properly managed credit accounts can create a more established credit history.

Your credit score is only one part of this picture. Your credit reports contain the underlying account information, while different scoring models turn some of that information into numerical scores.

How to Build Credit From Scratch

If you have never used credit, your first objective is usually to establish a manageable account that reports your payment activity to the major credit bureaus.

Options can include products specifically designed for people who are new to credit. Depending on your circumstances, these may include secured credit cards, certain starter credit cards, or credit-builder products.

Start small:

The goal is not to borrow as much as possible. The goal is to establish a credit history that you can manage without creating unnecessary debt.

Before opening an account, check:

  • Whether the account reports payment activity to credit bureaus.
  • Annual fees, interest rates, deposits, or other costs.
  • The credit limit and whether it fits your budget.
  • The account's payment terms and due date.
  • Whether the product is appropriate for your financial situation.

10 Steps to Build Credit

1

Get a credit account you can manage

If you do not have an established credit history, consider a credit product designed for your situation. Choose an account whose payments and fees fit comfortably within your budget.

2

Pay every bill on time

Payment history is an important component of many credit-scoring models. Set reminders or automatic payments so that required payments are made by their due dates.

3

Keep credit-card balances manageable

Credit utilization is the amount of revolving credit you are using compared with your available credit limits. Lower utilization can be beneficial to your credit profile, although there is no single percentage that guarantees a particular score.

4

Do not carry debt just for your score

Carrying a credit-card balance and paying interest is not generally required to build credit. You can use a card and pay the statement balance according to the card's terms.

5

Apply for new credit selectively

Opening several accounts in a short period can create multiple inquiries and new accounts. Apply when the account serves a genuine financial need.

6

Keep older accounts when appropriate

The length of your credit history can be considered by scoring models. Before closing an older account, consider its fees, usage, credit limit, and how closing it could affect your overall credit profile.

7

Monitor your credit reports

Review your credit reports periodically for accounts, balances, payment information, and other details that you do not recognize or believe are inaccurate.

8

Dispute inaccurate information

If your credit report contains information that is inaccurate or incomplete, follow the applicable dispute process with the credit reporting company and, when appropriate, the company that supplied the information.

9

Build an emergency fund

Savings do not directly create a credit score, but an emergency fund can make it easier to handle unexpected expenses without relying heavily on credit cards.

10

Give your credit history time

Credit history develops over time. There is no reliable shortcut that instantly creates a long record of responsible credit use.

1. Pay Your Bills on Time

Payment history is one of the major categories considered by commonly used credit-scoring models. A pattern of on-time payments can help establish a positive credit history.

Missing a payment can have consequences beyond a credit score. Depending on the account and circumstances, you could also face late fees, interest charges, or other account consequences.

Ways to avoid missed payments

  • Set up automatic payments for at least the required minimum when appropriate.
  • Create calendar reminders several days before due dates.
  • Keep sufficient funds available for scheduled automatic payments.
  • Check statements regularly for unexpected changes.

2. Manage Your Credit Utilization

Credit utilization compares revolving balances with available revolving credit. Credit-card utilization is an important consideration in many scoring models.

Example: Credit utilization

Total credit limits $10,000
Reported card balances $2,000
Utilization 20%

The basic calculation is:

Credit utilization = revolving balance ÷ total revolving credit limit × 100

Both overall utilization and the utilization on individual revolving accounts can matter to some scoring models.

There is no universal utilization percentage that guarantees a specific credit score. The scoring model and the rest of your credit profile also matter.

3. Do You Need to Carry a Credit-Card Balance?

No. You generally do not need to leave an unpaid credit-card balance from month to month just to build credit.

If your card issuer allows it and your budget permits, paying the statement balance by the due date can help you avoid interest on purchases under the card's terms while still allowing the account's activity to be reported.

Important distinction:

Having a balance appear on a statement is not the same as carrying debt from one billing cycle to the next. The reporting date and payment due date can be different.

4. Apply for New Credit Carefully

New credit can affect several parts of your credit profile. Applying for credit can result in a hard inquiry, and opening a new account can change the age and composition of your credit history.

That does not mean you should never apply for credit. It means that applications should generally be connected to a genuine need rather than an attempt to accumulate accounts simply for the sake of having more credit.

Before applying, ask:

  • Do I actually need this account?
  • Can I comfortably manage the payments?
  • What fees and interest rates apply?
  • Will opening the account change my budget?

Can a Secured Credit Card Help Build Credit?

A secured credit card can be an option for some people who are building or rebuilding credit. It typically requires a refundable security deposit that can serve as collateral for the account.

A secured card is still a credit account. You generally need to make payments according to the account terms, and fees or interest may apply.

What to check before choosing one

Check annual fees, application fees, maintenance fees, and other charges.

Graduation

Can Becoming an Authorized User Build Credit?

Becoming an authorized user on someone else's credit card can potentially add account information to your credit history, depending on the issuer and reporting practices.

This can be useful in some situations, but it is not automatically beneficial. The account's payment history and reported balances may affect the authorized user's credit profile.

Choose carefully:

If you become an authorized user, understand the account's payment history, balance management, and reporting practices before relying on it to build credit.

What Is a Credit-Builder Loan?

A credit-builder loan is a product designed to help establish or strengthen a credit history. Unlike a conventional loan where you typically receive the borrowed funds upfront, some credit-builder structures hold the loan proceeds while you make scheduled payments.

After the required payments are completed, the funds may be released according to the product's terms.

Because products differ, review the fees, payment schedule, interest cost, reporting practices, and account terms before opening one.

Check Your Credit Reports

Building credit is easier when you know what information is actually being reported about you.

Review your reports for:

  • Accounts you do not recognize.
  • Incorrect balances or account statuses.
  • Payment information that appears inaccurate.
  • Incorrect personal information.
  • Duplicate or outdated information that should be reviewed.

If you find inaccurate information, use the applicable dispute process rather than ignoring it.

How Long Does It Take to Build Credit?

There is no universal timeline for reaching a particular credit score. The amount of time required depends on factors such as whether you are starting with no credit history, what accounts are reported, payment history, balances, account age, and the scoring model being used.

Stage What may be happening
Starting out You may have little or no information available for some scoring models.
First reported accounts Payment and account information begins creating a credit history.
Several months Consistent payments and responsible balance management create more history.
Longer term Account age and a longer record of payment behavior can become part of the credit profile.

Credit building is therefore better viewed as an ongoing financial habit rather than a short-term challenge.

Credit-Building Mistakes to Avoid

!

Missing payments

A missed payment can affect your credit history and may also result in fees or other account consequences.

!

Maxing out cards

High revolving utilization can negatively affect scores under many scoring models.

!

Opening accounts unnecessarily

Applying for multiple accounts simply to increase the number of accounts you have can create unnecessary complexity.

!

Closing accounts without considering the impact

Closing an account can affect available credit, utilization, and other parts of your credit profile.

A Simple Credit-Building Checklist

If you want a simple starting point, work through these tasks before opening unnecessary accounts or making major changes.

✓ Check your credit reports and understand which accounts are currently reported.
✓ Set up payment reminders or automatic payments.
✓ Calculate your current credit-card utilization.
✓ Review recurring expenses and make sure credit payments fit your budget.
✓ Dispute any information you believe is inaccurate.
✓ Avoid applying for accounts that you do not actually need.

Can You Build Credit Without Going Into Debt?

Yes. Building credit does not require you to maintain expensive credit-card debt.

The objective is to establish a record of responsible credit management. If you use a credit card, you can generally avoid carrying a balance simply for the purpose of building credit.

You can also focus on keeping balances manageable, paying required amounts on time, and using credit only when it fits your overall budget.

How to Build Credit FAQs

How can I build credit from zero?

Start by establishing an appropriate credit account that reports to credit bureaus, then make payments on time, manage balances carefully, and maintain the account responsibly over time.

How long does it take to build credit?

There is no fixed timeline. Your credit history develops as accounts are reported and you demonstrate payment behavior over time.

Do I need a credit card to build credit?

No. A credit card is one possible way to establish credit, but other credit products may also be reported to credit bureaus.

Do I have to carry a balance to build credit?

No. Carrying an unpaid credit-card balance and paying interest is generally not required to build credit.

Does paying a credit card in full build credit?

Paying your credit-card statement balance in full according to the account terms can help you avoid carrying purchase debt while the account's payment activity may continue to contribute to your credit history.

What is the fastest way to build credit?

There is no guaranteed shortcut to building a strong credit history. Establishing an account that reports to credit bureaus and consistently managing it responsibly is the foundation.

Can a secured credit card build credit?

A secured credit card can help establish credit when the issuer reports the account to credit bureaus and the account is managed responsibly. Terms vary by issuer.

Can an authorized user build credit?

An authorized-user account can potentially appear on your credit history depending on the issuer's reporting practices. The account's history and balances may affect the reported profile.

Does checking my credit report hurt my score?

Checking your own credit report generally does not have the same effect as a hard inquiry from applying for credit.

Can I build credit without taking on debt?

Yes. You do not generally need to carry unpaid credit-card debt or pay interest simply to establish a credit history.
