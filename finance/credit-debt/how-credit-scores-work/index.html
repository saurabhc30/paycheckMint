How Credit Scores Work

A credit score is a number generated from information in your credit report. Learn what goes into a score, why different scores can exist, how credit utilization works, and what lenders may look at when you apply for credit.

Quick answer

A credit score is calculated from information in your credit report using a particular scoring model. Payment history, amounts owed, revolving credit utilization, length of credit history, types of credit, and recent credit activity can all play a role. The exact calculation varies by scoring model, so there is not one universal formula used for every credit decision.

What is a credit score?

A credit score is a numerical representation generated by a credit scoring model. The model analyzes information associated with your credit history and produces a score intended to help predict credit risk.

In the United States, consumers can have multiple credit scores at the same time. Different lenders may use different scoring models, credit reporting agencies, versions of a model, or information depending on the type of credit being considered.

That means the number you see when checking your credit does not necessarily have to be identical to the number a lender uses.

Credit score ≠ credit report

Your credit report contains the underlying credit information. A credit score is a number calculated from information using a particular scoring model.

How does the credit scoring process work?

The basic process can be thought of as three stages: information is reported, a scoring model evaluates that information, and a score is generated.

1

Credit information is reported

Creditors and other companies may report account information to consumer reporting agencies. The information can include account status, balances, payment history, and other details.

2

A scoring model evaluates it

A scoring model applies its own rules to the available information. Different models can treat the same information differently.

3

A score is produced

The result is a numerical score. Lenders can use this score alongside other information when evaluating an application.

4

The information can change

As creditors report updated balances, payments, accounts, or other information, a later calculation can produce a different score.

What factors affect a credit score?

Credit scoring models do not all use identical formulas. However, common scoring factors include payment history, amounts owed, credit history, types of credit, and recent credit activity.

Factor What it means Why it matters
Payment history Whether accounts have been paid on time or have reported late payments. A history of paying obligations as agreed can be an important part of credit scoring.
Amounts owed The balances you owe across credit accounts. Higher balances can indicate more credit exposure.
Revolving utilization How much revolving credit you are using relative to available limits. Utilization can change as card balances and limits change.
Length of history How long accounts and credit activity have been established. Longer credit histories can give a model more information about past credit behavior.
Credit mix The types of credit accounts in your profile. Some scoring models consider the variety of credit experience.
Recent credit activity New accounts and certain credit applications or inquiries. A pattern of seeking new credit can be relevant to some models.
There is no single universal percentage formula

You may see specific percentages online for particular scoring systems. Those figures should not be treated as a universal formula for every score. Different scoring models can weigh and evaluate information differently.

Why payment history matters

Payment history describes how you have handled payments on reported credit accounts. Depending on the scoring model, information about late or missed payments can affect a credit score.

A single late payment does not necessarily tell the whole story of a credit profile. The effect can depend on factors such as the account, the timing, the severity of the delinquency, and the scoring model.

Practical ways to avoid missed payments

  • Set up automatic payments for at least the required minimum when appropriate.
  • Use calendar or banking alerts before due dates.
  • Keep enough cash in the payment account to cover scheduled automatic payments.
  • Review statements so unexpected charges do not cause an account to become overdue.
  • If you are struggling to pay, contact the creditor before missing a payment to ask about available options.

What is credit utilization?

Credit utilization generally refers to the amount of revolving credit you are using compared with your available revolving credit limits.

Basic calculation
Utilization = Balance ÷ Credit Limit × 100

Example: A $600 credit-card balance on a $2,000 limit produces 30% utilization for that card at the time the balance is measured.

If you have multiple credit cards, you can also look at your overall revolving utilization by comparing total revolving balances with total revolving limits.

Example: two credit cards

Card 1 $1,200 / $2,000
Card 2 $200 / $3,000
Combined $1,400 / $5,000

The combined utilization in this example is 28%. The actual score impact depends on the scoring model and the rest of the credit profile.

Credit utilization can change without opening a new account. For example, paying down a card can reduce the reported balance, while a large purchase can increase it. The timing of when information is reported can therefore matter.

Credit report vs. credit score

These terms are often used together, but they are not the same thing.

Credit report Credit score
Contains credit-related information reported about you. A numerical result generated by a scoring model.
Can contain account details, payment history, balances, and inquiries. Summarizes risk-related information according to a particular model.
The underlying information can change as creditors report updates. Can change when the information used by the model changes.

Checking a report can therefore be useful even when you are not applying for credit. Errors in reported information can potentially affect how a scoring model evaluates your credit profile.

Why can I have more than one credit score?

There is no requirement that every lender use the same credit score. Several factors can cause the number you see to differ from another score.

  • Different scoring models: Models can use different formulas and evaluate information differently.
  • Different model versions: A newer or specialized version may produce a different result.
  • Different credit reports: Information available to one reporting agency may not be identical to information available to another.
  • Different timing: Account information can be updated at different times.
  • Different lending purposes: Some scoring models are designed for particular types of credit decisions.
Don't chase one specific number

A credit score is useful in context. Rather than assuming one score represents every lender's decision, focus on maintaining accurate credit information and responsible credit habits.

Hard inquiries vs. soft inquiries

A credit inquiry occurs when credit information is accessed. The effect depends on the type of inquiry and the circumstances.

H

Hard inquiry

A hard inquiry can occur when you apply for certain types of credit and authorize a lender or creditor to review your credit information. Hard inquiries can be considered by some scoring models.

S

Soft inquiry

Soft inquiries can occur in situations such as checking your own credit information or certain promotional or account-review situations. They are treated differently from hard inquiries for scoring purposes.

If you are shopping for credit, the treatment of multiple inquiries can depend on the scoring model and type of loan. It is useful to understand the lender's application process before submitting multiple applications.

How to build a stronger credit profile

Improving credit generally involves consistent financial habits rather than one quick fix. The steps below can help you focus on the information that commonly matters to credit scoring models.

1

Pay accounts on time

Payment history is an important part of many scoring models. Use automatic payments and reminders to reduce the chance of accidentally missing a due date.

2

Manage revolving balances

Paying down credit-card balances can reduce revolving utilization. Avoid carrying balances simply because you believe a balance is necessary to build credit.

3

Check your credit reports

Review your reports for incorrect account information, unfamiliar accounts, inaccurate payment status, or other errors.

4

Limit unnecessary new applications

Apply for credit when you have a genuine need rather than opening accounts only to increase your number of accounts.

5

Keep established accounts in context

Do not automatically close an older account simply because it has been paid off. Consider fees, usefulness, available credit, and the potential effects on your overall credit profile.

6

Use credit only as part of a sustainable budget

A budget can help prevent credit-card balances and missed payments from becoming recurring problems.

What if your credit score is low?

The right starting point depends on why the score is low. Someone with several missed payments may need a different strategy from someone whose main issue is high revolving utilization.

If missed payments are the problem

  • Bring past-due accounts current if possible.
  • Set up reminders or automatic payments.
  • Contact creditors if you are having difficulty making required payments.
  • Focus on avoiding additional late payments.

If high card balances are the problem

  • Continue making at least the required payments on all accounts.
  • Review your budget to determine how much extra cash can go toward debt.
  • Prioritize debt repayment according to your financial goals and interest costs.
  • Reduce unnecessary new charges while paying down balances.

If debt payments are consuming too much of your income, review your spending and cash flow. A practical starting point is the PaycheckMint budgeting guide .

What should you do if your credit report has an error?

Start by identifying exactly what information is inaccurate. Examples can include an account that does not belong to you, an incorrect payment status, an incorrect balance, or outdated personal information.

  1. Obtain and review your credit reports.
  2. Identify the specific information you believe is inaccurate or incomplete.
  3. Collect documents that support your position.
  4. Follow the applicable dispute process with the relevant credit reporting agency and, where appropriate, the company that supplied the information.
  5. Keep copies of your dispute and supporting documents.
An accurate negative item is different from an error

A dispute process is intended to address inaccurate or incomplete information. You should not assume that accurate negative information can simply be removed because it hurts your score.

How long does it take to improve a credit score?

There is no universal timeline. A score can change when new information is reported and incorporated into a scoring model, but the size and speed of a change depend on the underlying credit profile.

For example, reducing a reported revolving balance can affect a later score calculation relatively quickly once updated information is available. Rebuilding a history affected by repeated late payments can take considerably longer because it involves establishing a consistent record over time.

Be cautious of companies promising a guaranteed number of points or a guaranteed timeline. Credit scoring depends on the information in your profile and the model being used.

A simple 30-day credit checkup

You do not need to make dramatic changes all at once. Use the following checklist to understand where your credit profile stands.

✓ List every credit account, balance, credit limit, and payment due date.
✓ Set payment reminders or automatic payments.
✓ Review your credit reports for unfamiliar or inaccurate information.
✓ Calculate your revolving credit utilization.
✓ Choose a realistic amount of extra money to direct toward revolving debt if appropriate.
✓ Avoid unnecessary applications for new credit.
✓ Review your budget so future credit payments remain manageable.

Credit-score mistakes to avoid

  • Carrying a credit-card balance just to build credit. Paying interest is not generally required simply to establish responsible credit behavior.
  • Opening unnecessary accounts. More accounts do not automatically mean a better credit profile.
  • Maxing out available credit. High revolving utilization can be unfavorable under many scoring models.
  • Closing every paid-off card. Closing accounts can affect available credit and other aspects of your profile.
  • Disputing information simply because it is negative. Disputes are designed to address inaccurate or incomplete information.
  • Believing guaranteed score-increase claims. No legitimate strategy can guarantee a specific score increase for every consumer.

How credit scores work: FAQs

What is a credit score?

A credit score is a number generated by a credit scoring model using information associated with your credit history. It is designed to help lenders evaluate credit risk.

What factors affect a credit score?

Common factors include payment history, amounts owed, revolving utilization, length of credit history, types of credit, and recent credit activity. Their importance varies by scoring model.

Does checking my own credit score lower it?

Checking your own credit information is generally treated differently from a hard inquiry made in connection with a credit application. It does not normally have the same scoring treatment as a hard inquiry.

Does paying off a credit card improve your credit score?

Paying down a revolving balance can reduce reported utilization, which can be helpful under many scoring models. However, the exact score effect depends on the rest of the credit profile and the model being used.

What is a good credit utilization ratio?

Lower revolving utilization is generally viewed more favorably by many scoring models, but there is no universal utilization percentage that guarantees a particular score. The effect depends on the scoring model and overall profile.

How long does it take to improve credit?

It varies. Changes can occur as updated information is reported, while rebuilding a credit history affected by repeated negative information can take much longer.

Can I build credit without carrying a balance?

Yes. You generally do not need to pay credit-card interest just to demonstrate responsible credit use. Paying your required payments on time and managing credit responsibly can support a healthy credit profile.

Should I close a credit card after paying it off?

Not automatically. Consider annual fees, usefulness, available credit, account history, and how closing the account could affect your overall credit profile.

Can accurate negative information be removed from a credit report?

Accurate negative information generally cannot simply be removed because it is unfavorable. Dispute processes are intended to correct information that is inaccurate or incomplete.

Do I need a credit-repair company to improve my credit?

Not necessarily. Many basic credit management steps involve reviewing your reports, correcting inaccurate information, paying accounts on time, managing balances, and avoiding unnecessary new debt. Be cautious about companies promising guaranteed results.
