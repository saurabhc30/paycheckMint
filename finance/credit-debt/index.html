Credit refers to your ability and history of borrowing and repaying money, while debt is money you currently owe. Credit reports record information about your borrowing history, while credit scores use information from those reports to estimate credit risk. Understanding both can help you compare borrowing costs, monitor your financial accounts and make more informed decisions.

What Are Credit and Debt?

Credit generally refers to your ability to borrow money or access goods and services with an agreement to pay later. Your credit history records how you have handled borrowing and payments over time.

Debt is money you have borrowed and still owe. Credit cards, auto loans, mortgages, student loans and other borrowing arrangements can create debt that must be repaid according to the terms of the agreement.

Credit and debt are connected, but they are not the same thing. You can have available credit without carrying a balance. Using credit responsibly can also help establish a positive credit history.

Credit is not the same as your credit score.

A credit score is a number generated by a particular scoring model. Your credit report contains the underlying information that a scoring model may use.

Why Does Credit Matter?

Your credit history and scores can affect important financial decisions. Lenders may use credit information when deciding whether to approve an application and what interest rate or terms to offer.

Credit information can also matter in some housing, insurance, utility and other financial or service decisions. The exact use depends on the company, product and applicable laws.

Credit scores are designed to help estimate the likelihood that a borrower will repay borrowed money as agreed. Different lenders may use different scoring models, so the score you see from one source may not be identical to a score used in another lending decision.

1 Credit history can affect access to certain borrowing products.
2 Credit scores can influence the terms offered by some lenders.
3 Credit reports can contain errors worth identifying and correcting.
4 Interest rates can make different types of debt cost very different amounts.

Credit Scores vs. Credit Reports

A credit report and a credit score are related, but they serve different purposes.

Credit report Credit score
A record containing information about your credit history. A number generated by a specific scoring model using information from a credit file.
Can include accounts, payment information and identifying information. Summarizes information into a numerical estimate of credit risk.
Information can differ between reporting companies. Scores can differ because lenders may use different models and versions.

What Can Affect a Credit Score?

Credit scoring models can consider different information, depending on the model being used. Common areas include:

  • Payment history
  • Current debt and account balances
  • How much available revolving credit is being used
  • Length of credit history
  • Recent applications for credit
  • Types and number of credit accounts
  • Certain negative information such as collections or bankruptcy

Payment history and revolving credit usage are important areas to understand, but the exact effect of any action depends on the scoring model and the information in your credit file.

You do not have just one credit score.

Different scoring models and data sources can produce different scores. A score you see from one service may therefore differ from a score a lender uses.

Why Should You Check Your Credit Report?

Reviewing your credit report can help you identify inaccurate information and recognize accounts or activity you do not recognize.

Consumers can obtain official credit reports through AnnualCreditReport.com .

When reviewing a report, look for:

✓ Incorrect personal information
✓ Accounts that are not yours
✓ Incorrect payment information
✓ Duplicate or incomplete information

If you find information that appears inaccurate, review the dispute process provided by the relevant credit reporting company and the company that supplied the information.

Understanding Debt

Debt is not automatically harmful. Borrowing can help people finance purchases or expenses that would otherwise require significant cash upfront.

The more important questions are how much you owe, the interest rate, the required payment, the remaining repayment period and whether the payment fits comfortably within your budget.

Different debts can have very different costs. A credit card balance, for example, can accrue interest differently from an installment loan.

Before taking on or keeping debt, consider:

  • Total balance owed
  • Annual percentage rate or other applicable interest rate
  • Required monthly payment
  • Remaining repayment period
  • Fees and penalties
  • Whether the debt is secured or unsecured
  • How the payment fits into your monthly budget

How Credit Card Interest Fits In

Credit card interest can make carrying a balance more expensive when you do not pay the required amount according to the card's terms.

Many card issuers calculate interest using a daily method based on an average daily balance, although the exact calculation depends on the card agreement.

A card may also provide a grace period for purchases. When a grace period applies and you pay the required balance in full by the due date, you may avoid interest on eligible purchases. Cash advances and other transaction types can have different rates or rules.

Key idea
Higher APR + Longer Balance = Higher Potential Interest Cost
Your actual cost depends on the card agreement, balance, payments and interest calculation.

Understanding interest before choosing a repayment strategy can help you see why paying down high-interest balances may reduce the total cost of borrowing.

Managing and Paying Off Debt

A debt payoff plan starts with knowing what you owe and how much each balance costs you.

1
List every debt Record each current balance and account.
2
Record interest rates Note the APR or applicable interest rate for each balance.
3
Record minimum payments Know the required payment for every account.
4
Review your budget Compare income and essential expenses before choosing an extra payment amount.
5
Choose a repayment approach Select a method that fits your finances and that you can consistently follow.

Two common approaches are the debt snowball, which generally focuses extra payments on the smallest balance first, and the debt avalanche, which generally focuses extra payments on the highest-interest debt first.

The two methods organize extra payments differently. The important practical step is to keep required minimum payments current while directing available extra money according to your chosen strategy.

What Is Debt-to-Income Ratio?

Debt-to-income ratio, commonly called DTI, compares your monthly debt payments with your gross monthly income.

DTI formula
DTI = Monthly Debt Payments ÷ Gross Monthly Income × 100
Example: $2,000 in monthly debt payments ÷ $6,000 gross monthly income = 33.3% DTI.

Lenders can use DTI as one measure of a borrower's ability to manage monthly payments. Requirements can vary by lender, loan type and other factors.

DTI is different from credit utilization. DTI compares debt payments with income, while credit utilization compares revolving credit balances with available revolving credit.

Practical Credit Habits

There is no single action that guarantees a particular credit score. However, several habits can help you manage credit responsibly.

✓ Pay bills on time.
✓ Monitor your credit reports for errors.
✓ Avoid applying for credit you do not need.
✓ Keep revolving balances manageable.
✓ Understand interest rates before carrying balances.
✓ Consider the consequences before closing older accounts.

Credit & Debt FAQs

Credit refers to your ability or history of borrowing and repaying money. Debt is money that you currently owe. You can have available credit without carrying a debt balance.
A credit score is a number generated by a particular scoring model using information from a credit report to estimate credit risk. Different scoring models can produce different scores.
A credit report is a record of information about your credit history maintained by credit reporting companies. It can include account information, payment history and other identifying details.
Reviewing your credit reports regularly can help you identify inaccurate information and unfamiliar accounts. Official credit reports are available through AnnualCreditReport.com.
You generally do not need to carry a credit card balance or pay interest simply to build a credit history. Paying according to your account terms and managing credit responsibly can support positive credit behavior.

Credit utilization compares revolving credit balances with available revolving credit. It is one of the credit-related measurements that can be considered by certain credit scoring models.
