How the biweekly paycheck calculator works

A biweekly paycheck is paid every two weeks, which gives 26 regular pay periods in a typical year. The IRS payroll tables in Publication 15-T include biweekly payroll as a 26-period schedule.

This calculator starts with your gross pay and estimates federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, state and local taxes, and the deductions you enter. It shows what may reach your bank account from a normal biweekly paycheck.

What taxes reduce your paycheck?

Federal income tax withholding depends on your wages and the information on your Form W-4; the IRS explains the process in Publication 15. Social Security (6.2%, up to a $184,500 wage base in 2026) and Medicare (1.45%) are separate payroll taxes. Social Security wage limits are published by the Social Security Administration.

2026 federal tax assumptions

For 2026 this calculator uses a standard deduction of $16,100 for single filers, $24,150 for heads of household and $32,200 for married couples filing jointly, with seven marginal rates from 10% to 37% and a $2,200 credit per qualifying child. It annualizes your taxable wages, applies those brackets and divides the result across 26 paychecks. Health, HSA and FSA contributions also reduce your FICA wages.

Biweekly vs. semimonthly pay

Schedule Payments Checks per year Gross on $65,000
Biweekly Every 2 weeks 26 $2,500.00
Semimonthly Twice per month 24 $2,708.33

Biweekly checks are smaller but you get two bonus paychecks in most years. Semimonthly checks are larger and arrive on the same dates each month, such as the 15th and the last day.

Why your biweekly paycheck may change

Your paycheck can change even when your salary stays the same. Common causes are changes to benefits, retirement contributions, tax withholding, overtime, bonuses and other payroll deductions. The IRS suggests checking your withholding with its Tax Withholding Estimator after major life or income changes.

Common mistakes when estimating biweekly take-home pay

  • Dividing annual salary by 24 instead of 26.
  • Treating a biweekly schedule as the same thing as semimonthly pay.
  • Forgetting retirement or insurance deductions.
  • Using an annual tax amount as if it were a single-paycheck deduction.
  • Ignoring state or local income taxes.
  • Assuming the estimate exactly matches an employer's payroll system.

Frequently asked questions

How many biweekly paychecks are there in a year?

Most years have 26 biweekly paychecks because employees are paid every two weeks. Occasionally a year has 27, when paydays happen to fall on the first days of the calendar year. Use the 3-paycheck month finder on this page to check your own calendar.

How do I calculate biweekly pay from an annual salary?

Divide your annual gross salary by 26. For example, a $78,000 salary produces $3,000 in gross pay per regular biweekly paycheck before taxes and deductions.

How much is a $60,000 salary biweekly after taxes?

A $60,000 salary is about $2,307.69 gross per biweekly paycheck. After federal income tax and FICA for a single filer with no other deductions, take-home is roughly $1,938.08, before any state or local tax.

Is biweekly the same as twice a month?

No. Biweekly means every two weeks and normally produces 26 paychecks a year. Semimonthly means twice a month, usually on set dates, and produces 24. On a $65,000 salary, a biweekly check is $2,500 gross and a semimonthly check is $2,708.33 gross.

Which months have three biweekly paychecks?

Two months each year usually have three paychecks, depending on your payday. Enter any of your paydays in the finder above to see which months they are in 2026 or 2027.

How do I calculate biweekly pay from an hourly rate?

Multiply your hourly rate by the hours worked in the two-week period. For a standard 80-hour period, $25 an hour is $2,000 gross. Add overtime hours at 1.5 times your rate where they apply.

Does this calculator include state tax?

Yes, it uses simplified average effective state rates for planning, plus any local tax rate you enter. Real state withholding uses brackets, deductions and credits, so use your pay stub or your state's calculator for exact figures.

Why does my actual paycheck differ from the calculator?

Payroll withholding depends on your Form W-4, benefits, deductions, payroll system, state and local rules and other circumstances. Withholding is an estimate of your tax, not your final tax bill.

Actual withholding may differ based on your employer's payroll system, Form W-4 elections, tax credits, benefits and state and local rules.
