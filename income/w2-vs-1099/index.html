W-2 vs 1099: What's the Difference?

W-2 employees and 1099 independent contractors can earn similar amounts of money, but the way they are paid, taxed, classified, and provided benefits can be very different.

Quick answer: A W-2 generally means you work as an employee and your employer handles payroll withholding. A 1099 arrangement generally means you work as an independent contractor and are responsible for managing your own taxes and business expenses.

W-2 vs 1099 at a glance

The biggest difference between W-2 and 1099 work is the relationship between the worker and the business paying them. A W-2 worker is generally an employee. A person receiving contractor compensation reported on Form 1099-NEC is generally working as an independent contractor.

This distinction affects more than the tax form you receive. It can affect payroll withholding, Social Security and Medicare taxes, employee benefits, business expenses, recordkeeping, and how you plan for your tax bill.

Worker status Generally an employee Generally an independent contractor
How you're paid Usually through payroll Usually through contractor payments or invoices
Income tax withholding Generally withheld from paychecks Generally not withheld from ordinary contractor payments

What is a W-2 employee?

A W-2 employee works for an employer as part of an employment relationship. The employer generally runs payroll, withholds applicable taxes from wages, and reports the employee's annual wages and withholding on Form W-2.

Your paycheck may therefore show several amounts being withheld before you receive your take-home pay. Federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and possibly state or local taxes can affect the amount deposited into your bank account.

💼

Employee structure

The employer generally determines important parts of the work relationship, including duties, schedules, procedures, and how work is performed.

💰

Payroll withholding

Taxes are generally withheld from each paycheck, making tax payments less visible than they are for many independent contractors.

What is a 1099 independent contractor?

An independent contractor generally operates independently rather than as an employee of the business paying them. Freelancers, consultants, certain gig workers, and other self-employed individuals may receive contractor income.

Instead of receiving a traditional payroll paycheck, a contractor may invoice a client or receive payments according to an independent contractor agreement.

Contractor income is often received before federal income tax and self-employment tax are set aside. This means the amount deposited into your account should not automatically be treated as your spendable take-home pay.

Important: Getting a 1099 does not by itself determine whether someone is legally an independent contractor. Worker classification depends on the facts of the working relationship, including the degree of control and independence involved.

W-2 vs 1099 taxes

Taxes are one of the most important differences to understand. The amount you receive from a client or employer is not necessarily the same as the amount you can safely spend.

How taxes generally work for W-2 employees

A W-2 employee generally has taxes withheld from each paycheck. The employer also generally has its own payroll-tax responsibilities.

This withholding is one reason a W-2 employee may receive a smaller net paycheck than their stated gross wage.

How taxes generally work for 1099 contractors

Independent contractors generally receive payments without the same regular payroll withholding that applies to employees. They may therefore need to set aside money for federal and state taxes and, when applicable, make estimated tax payments.

Self-employment tax can also apply to net earnings from self-employment. The actual tax situation depends on the person's income, expenses, filing situation, and other factors.

Planning tip: If you are paid as a contractor, consider treating part of every payment as money reserved for taxes rather than as immediately available spending money.

Why a $60,000 W-2 job isn't necessarily equal to $60,000 of 1099 income

Comparing only the headline income can be misleading. Suppose two opportunities both advertise $60,000 per year. One is a W-2 position and the other pays $60,000 as an independent contractor.

The contractor may need to account for taxes, health insurance, retirement savings, software, equipment, professional fees, unpaid administrative time, and other costs that an employee may receive partially or fully from an employer.

At the same time, contractor work can offer greater flexibility and may allow legitimate business expenses to be considered when determining taxable business income.

The practical comparison is therefore the total compensation and total costs, not just the number shown on an offer letter or invoice.

Benefits can make a substantial difference when comparing employment arrangements.

How to compare a W-2 offer with a 1099 offer

If you have a choice between employment and contract work, compare the complete financial picture rather than focusing only on hourly or annual pay.

1

Compare gross compensation

Start with the annual or hourly amount each opportunity actually pays.

2

Account for benefits

Estimate the value of health insurance, retirement contributions, paid time off, bonuses, and other benefits.

3

Estimate taxes

Consider income tax, payroll or self-employment tax, and state or local taxes that may apply.

4

Subtract work-related costs

Consider equipment, insurance, software, professional services, travel, and other costs required to earn the income.

5

Compare flexibility

Consider scheduling freedom, client requirements, job stability, and how much control you have over your work.

Can you have both W-2 and 1099 income?

Yes. It is possible to have a regular W-2 job and also earn money from freelance work, consulting, a side business, or another independent contracting activity.

In that situation, your W-2 income and contractor income are handled differently for tax purposes. Good recordkeeping is especially important because you need to track your business income and eligible expenses separately.

This arrangement is common among people who use freelance work or a side hustle to create an additional source of income.

Common W-2 vs 1099 mistakes

  • Comparing only the advertised pay: Benefits, taxes, expenses, and unpaid time can change the real value of an offer.
  • Spending all contractor payments: A 1099 payment may include money that needs to be reserved for taxes.
  • Ignoring business expenses: Contractors should maintain appropriate records of business-related expenses.
  • Assuming the form determines classification: Worker classification depends on the actual working relationship, not simply the tax form issued.
  • Forgetting benefits: Health insurance, retirement contributions, paid leave, and other benefits can have significant value.

W-2 vs 1099: Which should you choose?

There is no universal answer. The better fit depends on the specific compensation package, taxes, benefits, expenses, flexibility, stability, and work arrangement.

A W-2 arrangement can make payroll and tax withholding more straightforward and may include employer-sponsored benefits. A 1099 arrangement can provide more independence and may make sense for people who operate a genuine independent business.

Before comparing two opportunities, calculate the complete financial picture rather than treating the W-2 and 1099 headline amounts as directly interchangeable.

Frequently asked questions

Is a W-2 better than a 1099?

Neither arrangement is automatically better. W-2 employment may provide payroll withholding and employer benefits, while 1099 work may provide greater independence. Compare the complete compensation, expenses, taxes, and work arrangement.

Do 1099 workers pay more taxes?

A 1099 contractor generally handles their own income tax payments and may owe self-employment tax. The actual amount depends on income, expenses, filing situation, and other factors.

Can you be W-2 and 1099 at the same time?

Yes. You can have a W-2 job while also earning independent contractor income from freelance work, consulting, or another business activity.

Is 1099 income the same as salary?

No. 1099 contractor income is generally not treated the same way as wages from an employer. Contractors generally have additional responsibility for taxes and business expenses.

Can a 1099 contractor be paid hourly?

Yes. An independent contractor can be paid by the hour, by the project, by a milestone, or under another agreed payment structure.

Does receiving a 1099 automatically make someone a contractor?

No. The actual facts and circumstances of the working relationship are important when determining whether someone is an employee or an independent contractor.
