Salary vs. Hourly Pay: What's the Difference?

Salary and hourly pay are two common ways employees are compensated. Learn how they work, how to compare them, how to convert salary to hourly pay, how overtime can affect earnings, and how gross pay eventually becomes take-home pay.

Calculate Salary Pay Calculate Hourly Pay
Quick answer

Salary pay is generally a fixed amount of compensation for a year of work, usually divided among regular pay periods. Hourly pay is based on an hourly rate multiplied by the hours worked. The actual amount an employee receives can also be affected by overtime, bonuses, unpaid time, benefits, deductions, and tax withholding.

The most useful way to compare salary and hourly pay is not simply to compare the headline numbers. You should also consider expected working hours, overtime eligibility, pay frequency, benefits, deductions, schedule, and estimated take-home pay.

What is the difference between salary and hourly pay?

Salary and hourly pay describe different compensation structures. With a salary, an employee generally receives a predetermined annual amount that is distributed across paychecks. With hourly pay, compensation is calculated using an hourly rate and the number of hours worked.

For example, an employee with a $60,000 annual salary may have that amount divided across 12 monthly, 24 semi-monthly, or 26 biweekly paychecks, depending on the employer's payroll schedule.

An employee earning $30 per hour, on the other hand, earns $30 for each regular hour worked before taxes and other deductions. If the employee works a different number of hours from one week to another, gross earnings can change.

How salary pay works

A salary is typically stated as an annual gross amount. Instead of recalculating the employee's regular pay from hours every pay period, the annual amount is divided according to the employer's payroll schedule.

For example, a $72,000 annual salary could produce approximately the following gross amounts before deductions:

Pay frequency Approximate gross paycheck
Weekly $1,384.62
Biweekly $2,769.23
Semi-monthly $3,000.00
Monthly $6,000.00

These figures represent gross pay before federal and state withholding, Social Security and Medicare taxes, retirement contributions, insurance premiums, and other deductions.

How hourly pay works

Hourly employees are generally paid according to an hourly rate and the number of hours worked. If the number of hours changes, gross pay can change as well.

Basic hourly pay formula
Hourly Rate × Hours Worked = Gross Pay

For example, someone earning $25 per hour who works 40 regular hours in a week would have $1,000 in regular gross pay for that week before deductions.

Example: $25 per hour

Hourly rate $25
Regular weekly hours 40 hours
Weekly gross pay $1,000
Estimated annual gross at 2,080 hours $52,000

How to calculate salary vs. hourly pay

Comparing annual salary and hourly pay requires an estimate of how many hours are actually worked during the year.

Salary to hourly calculation

Common estimate
Annual Salary ÷ Annual Hours Worked = Approximate Hourly Rate

A common full-time illustration uses 2,080 hours: 40 hours per week × 52 weeks = 2,080 hours.

Hourly to annual salary calculation

Common estimate
Hourly Rate × Hours per Week × Weeks per Year = Annual Gross Pay

The result is an estimate. Actual annual earnings can be different because employees may work fewer or more hours, take unpaid time, receive overtime, receive bonuses, or have other forms of compensation.

Salary vs. hourly pay and overtime

Overtime is one of the most important factors to consider when comparing compensation structures.

It is not accurate to assume that every salaried employee is exempt from overtime or that every hourly employee automatically receives overtime. Eligibility depends on the employee's classification and applicable federal, state, and local rules.

Employer policies, job duties, classification, and the applicable wage-and-hour rules can all affect overtime treatment.

Illustrative overtime calculation

Suppose an employee earns $30 per hour and is eligible for overtime under an applicable rule that uses a 1.5× overtime rate. The illustrative overtime rate would be:

Example only
$30 × 1.5 = $45/hour

Five overtime hours at that illustrative rate would add $225 to gross pay. Actual overtime treatment depends on the applicable rules and employee classification.

Benefits, deductions, and taxes

Comparing a salary and an hourly rate using gross pay alone can leave out important parts of total compensation.

Health insurance

Employee insurance premiums can reduce the amount of each paycheck and vary by employer and plan.

Retirement contributions

Contributions to an employer retirement plan can affect taxable wages and take-home pay depending on the plan.

Tax withholding

Federal, state, and applicable local withholding can reduce gross pay before the employee receives a paycheck.

Other deductions

Wage garnishments, benefit premiums, voluntary deductions, and other payroll items can affect net pay.

Because these factors vary by person and employer, two employees with the same gross salary or hourly earnings can have different take-home pay.

Does salary vs. hourly affect take-home pay?

Salary or hourly status by itself does not determine how much money reaches your bank account. Take-home pay starts with gross earnings and then accounts for applicable taxes and deductions.

Common paycheck items can include federal income tax withholding, Social Security, Medicare, state income tax where applicable, local taxes, health insurance, retirement contributions, and other deductions.

This means an employee should compare the complete compensation and paycheck picture rather than only the salary number or hourly rate.

What can change the actual value of a salary or hourly job?

A headline compensation figure does not tell the entire story. When comparing job offers or estimating annual earnings, consider these factors:

  • Expected hours worked each week
  • Overtime eligibility and applicable overtime rules
  • Paid and unpaid time off
  • Bonuses, commissions, or other additional compensation
  • Health insurance and other employee benefits
  • Employer retirement contributions
  • Employee payroll deductions
  • Federal, state, and local tax withholding
  • Work schedule and variability in hours
  • Employment classification and applicable wage-and-hour rules

Looking at these details can provide a more complete picture of compensation than comparing $X per hour with $Y per year alone.

Frequently asked questions

Salary pay is generally a fixed annual amount divided across pay periods, while hourly pay is based on an hourly rate multiplied by hours worked. Actual earnings can also be affected by overtime, bonuses, unpaid time, benefits, and deductions.

Divide the annual salary by the expected annual hours worked. A common full-time estimate uses 2,080 hours. For example, $50,000 divided by 2,080 is approximately $24.04 per hour.

Multiply the hourly rate by expected weekly hours and the number of weeks worked per year. For example, $25 per hour × 40 hours × 52 weeks equals $52,000 before deductions.

Generally, regular hourly earnings are calculated using the employee's hourly rate and compensable hours. Payroll treatment can vary depending on employment terms and applicable rules.

Some salaried employees may be eligible for overtime. Overtime eligibility depends on employee classification and applicable federal, state, and local rules rather than salary status alone.

Some hourly employees are eligible for overtime, but eligibility depends on classification and applicable wage-and-hour rules. Employer policies can also affect compensation.

The compensation structure itself does not determine take-home pay. Net pay depends on gross earnings, withholding, payroll taxes, benefits, retirement contributions, and other deductions.

A fixed salary can make gross income more predictable when the salary does not change between pay periods. Hourly income can vary when the number of hours worked changes.

Neither structure is automatically better for every employee. The practical difference depends on the pay rate, expected hours, overtime eligibility, benefits, deductions, schedule, and other employment terms.
