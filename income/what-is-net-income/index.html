What Is Net Income?

Net income is the amount left after applicable taxes, deductions, and expenses are taken out of income. Learn how net income works, how to calculate it, and why it matters when you are budgeting or comparing your pay.

Quick answer: Net income is generally what remains after applicable taxes, deductions, and expenses are subtracted from income. For an employee paycheck, net income is commonly discussed as net pay or take-home pay.

Net income meaning

Net income is generally the amount of income that remains after applicable taxes, deductions, and expenses have been subtracted.

When you look at an employee paycheck, your gross pay is the starting amount. Federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, state or local taxes, insurance premiums, retirement contributions, and other deductions may reduce that amount.

The amount that remains after those applicable deductions is generally your net pay, which is also commonly called your take-home pay.

Simple example: If your gross paycheck is $3,000 and $700 is withheld or deducted for taxes and other deductions, your net pay would be $2,300.

Net income formula

The exact meaning of net income depends on the context. For a simple employee paycheck calculation, the relationship can be expressed as:

Basic paycheck formula
Net Pay = Gross Pay − Taxes − Deductions

This is a simplified formula. Actual payroll calculations can contain several types of taxes, pre-tax deductions, post-tax deductions, benefits, retirement contributions, and other adjustments.

How net income is calculated

For an employee, the process generally begins with gross earnings. Payroll then applies applicable taxes and deductions before arriving at the amount paid to the employee.

1

Start with gross pay

Begin with your wages or salary before applicable deductions.

2

Subtract taxes

Applicable federal, payroll, state, and local taxes can reduce your paycheck.

3

Subtract deductions

Benefits, retirement contributions, and other deductions can further reduce the amount paid.

Gross income vs net income

Gross income and net income describe different points in the income calculation. Gross income generally represents income before applicable deductions, while net income represents what remains afterward.

Category Gross income Net income
Basic meaning Income before applicable taxes and deductions Income remaining after applicable deductions
Paycheck Gross pay or gross earnings Net pay or take-home pay
Taxes Before applicable tax withholding After applicable tax withholding
Deductions Before deductions are subtracted After applicable deductions
Budgeting Useful for understanding total earnings More useful for everyday spending plans

Net income vs net pay vs take-home pay

These terms are closely related, but they can have slightly different meanings depending on the financial context.

💵

Net pay

The amount left from an employee's gross paycheck after applicable taxes and payroll deductions.

🏦

Take-home pay

The amount you actually receive or have deposited after applicable paycheck deductions.

📊

Net income

A broader term that generally refers to income left after applicable expenses, taxes, and deductions.

🧾

Gross income

Income before applicable taxes, deductions, or expenses are subtracted.

What reduces your paycheck before you receive it?

Several items can reduce gross pay before money reaches your bank account. The exact deductions depend on your employer, benefits, tax situation, location, and elections.

  • Federal income tax: Federal income tax withholding may be taken from your paycheck.
  • Social Security tax: Employee payroll taxes can reduce your paycheck.
  • Medicare tax: Medicare payroll tax is generally withheld from employee wages.
  • State and local taxes: Depending on where you work and live, state or local withholding may apply.
  • Health insurance: Employee-paid premiums can reduce take-home pay.
  • Retirement contributions: Contributions to employer retirement plans can reduce the amount deposited into your account.
  • Other deductions: Certain benefits, insurance, garnishments, or other authorized deductions may also affect net pay.

Net income example

Suppose an employee has a gross paycheck of $4,000. Their paycheck contains the following simplified deductions:

Paycheck item Amount
Gross pay $4,000
Federal tax and other payroll taxes − $650
Insurance − $150
Retirement contribution − $200
Estimated net pay $3,000
Simplified calculation
$4,000 − $650 − $150 − $200 = $3,000

This example is simplified and does not represent an exact payroll tax calculation. Actual withholding can differ significantly based on the employee's circumstances.

Why is net income important?

Net income is one of the most useful numbers to understand when managing everyday finances because it gives you a clearer picture of the money available after applicable deductions.

1

Building a budget

Your regular take-home pay is generally more useful for budgeting than your gross salary because it reflects the amount actually available after paycheck deductions.

2

Comparing job offers

Two jobs with the same gross salary can produce different take-home pay because benefits, retirement contributions, taxes, and other deductions may differ.

3

Planning savings

Knowing your typical net pay makes it easier to determine how much can realistically go toward savings and financial goals.

4

Managing monthly expenses

Rent, utilities, groceries, debt payments, and other recurring expenses need to fit within the money you actually receive.

Net income and budgeting

A common budgeting mistake is using gross income as though it were the amount available for spending.

For example, if your annual salary is $72,000, your monthly gross salary may be $6,000. But your actual monthly take-home pay could be considerably lower after taxes, insurance, retirement contributions, and other deductions.

For everyday budgeting, your normal net pay is therefore an important number to know.

Practical rule: Use gross income to understand your earnings and compensation. Use your regular net pay or take-home pay to understand how much money is actually available for your spending plan.

How to find net income on a pay stub

Your pay stub normally separates earnings, taxes, deductions, and the amount paid to you. The exact labels vary by employer and payroll provider.

Look for terms such as:

  • Net pay
  • Net earnings
  • Take-home pay
  • Amount paid
  • Direct deposit amount

If you are reviewing a paycheck, remember to distinguish the current-period amount from the year-to-date amount. Your pay stub may show both.

Net income for hourly workers

For an hourly worker, net income starts with the gross wages earned during the pay period.

A simplified calculation might look like this:

Hourly paycheck
Hourly Rate × Hours Worked = Gross Pay

Taxes and deductions are then applied to arrive at an estimated net paycheck.

Overtime, bonuses, commissions, or changes in hours can cause gross pay and therefore net pay to change from one paycheck to another.

Net income for salaried workers

Salaried workers typically have a stated annual salary. However, the annual salary is not the same as the amount deposited into the employee's bank account.

For example, a $78,000 annual salary could be paid over 12 monthly paychecks, 24 semimonthly paychecks, or 26 biweekly paychecks.

Simplified annual-to-paycheck calculation
Gross Pay per Check = Annual Salary ÷ Number of Pay Periods

Taxes and deductions are then applied to each paycheck to determine the employee's net pay.

Net income vs adjusted gross income

Net income and adjusted gross income (AGI) are not the same thing.

AGI is a specific federal tax concept. It is calculated from gross income after certain adjustments permitted under tax rules. Net income, meanwhile, is commonly used to describe the amount remaining after applicable deductions or expenses, depending on the context.

This distinction matters when reading tax forms because a number shown on a paycheck should not automatically be treated as your AGI or taxable income.

Important: Gross pay, net pay, taxable income, and adjusted gross income are different concepts. The number you see on your paycheck is not automatically the same number used for every tax calculation.

Common net income mistakes

  • Confusing gross pay with net pay: Gross pay is before applicable deductions, while net pay is the amount remaining afterward.
  • Budgeting from gross salary: Your full salary is not necessarily the amount available for monthly spending.
  • Ignoring benefits: Health insurance and retirement contributions can reduce the amount deposited into your account.
  • Forgetting pay frequency: A monthly, semimonthly, and biweekly paycheck can have different gross amounts even when annual salary is the same.
  • Confusing net income with taxable income: These terms can describe different concepts and should not automatically be treated as interchangeable.

How to increase your net income

Your net income can potentially change when your earnings, tax withholding, or deductions change. Some changes are straightforward, while others depend on your individual circumstances.

📈

Increase earnings

A raise, additional hours, overtime, commission, or another income source can increase gross earnings.

🧾

Review deductions

Understand which benefits and voluntary deductions are reducing each paycheck.

🏦

Review benefits

Compare the cost and value of employer benefits during enrollment or when changing jobs.

🔎

Check your pay stub

Reviewing your deductions can help you understand why your net paycheck changes.
