How to Read a Paystub

A paystub shows more than your paycheck amount. Learn how to understand your pay period, gross pay, earnings, taxes, pre-tax and post-tax deductions, year-to-date totals, and net pay.

Calculate Take-Home Pay See a Sample Paystub
Quick answer
To read a paystub, start with the pay period and pay date. Then review your earnings, gross pay, taxable wages if shown, tax withholdings, other deductions, YTD totals, and finally your net pay. Net pay is the amount actually paid to you.

Paystub layouts differ between employers and payroll systems, so your statement may not use exactly the same labels or sections shown in this guide. The underlying information is usually similar: how much you earned, what was withheld or deducted, and how much you received.

How to Read a Paystub Step by Step

You do not need to understand every abbreviation immediately. Read your paystub from top to bottom and connect each section to the final net-pay number.

  1. Check your personal and employer information. Make sure your name and other identifying information are correct.
  2. Check the pay period and pay date. Confirm which dates the paycheck covers and when the payment was issued.
  3. Review your earnings. Check regular hours, overtime, pay rates, bonuses, commissions, vacation pay, or other earnings.
  4. Find gross pay. This is the total earnings before the applicable taxes and deductions are removed.
  5. Review taxes. Look for federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and applicable state or local taxes.
  6. Review other deductions. These may include health insurance, retirement contributions, flexible spending accounts, garnishments, or other deductions.
  7. Check YTD totals. Compare the cumulative numbers with previous paystubs when appropriate.
  8. Confirm net pay. This should correspond to the amount actually paid through your paycheck or direct deposit.

1. Check the Paystub Header

The top portion of a paystub commonly identifies the employee, employer, pay period, payment date, and sometimes a check number or employee ID.

Employee information Your name and, depending on the employer, address or employee ID.
Employer information Company, department, location, or payroll information.
Pay period The dates covered by the earnings on the statement.
Pay date The date the payment is issued or becomes available.
Pay period is not always the same as pay date. The pay period tells you when the work or earnings were calculated. The pay date tells you when the payment is issued.

2. Understand Your Earnings

The earnings section explains how your gross pay was generated. What appears here depends on whether you are paid hourly, salaried, or receive other types of compensation.

Hourly employees

An hourly paystub may show the number of hours worked, the hourly rate, and the resulting earnings.

Hours Worked × Hourly Rate = Regular Earnings Overtime or other pay may be calculated separately.

Salaried employees

A salaried employee may see a salary amount allocated to the current pay period rather than a simple hours-times-rate calculation.

Other earnings

Depending on your job and employer, the earnings section may include overtime, bonuses, commissions, holiday pay, vacation pay, sick pay, shift differentials, retroactive pay, or other compensation.

3. Find Your Gross Pay

Gross pay is the amount you earned before applicable taxes and other deductions are taken out.

It may include regular wages as well as overtime, bonuses, commissions, or other compensation. The exact items included depend on your pay and payroll system.

Important: Gross pay is not the same as take-home pay. Your bank account normally receives the net-pay amount, not your gross-pay amount.

4. Understand Taxable Wages

Some paystubs show one or more taxable-wage amounts in addition to gross pay. These numbers can be different because certain deductions or benefits may receive different tax treatment.

For example, a qualifying pre-tax benefit may reduce the wages subject to a particular income tax while not necessarily changing the wages subject to every payroll tax.

That is why you should not assume that one number on a paystub is the taxable amount for every tax.

Look for the tax-specific wage base. If your paystub lists federal taxable wages, Social Security wages, or Medicare wages separately, each figure may be used for a different payroll calculation.

This distinction is one reason gross pay, taxable income, and take-home pay should not be treated as interchangeable terms.

5. Read the Tax Withholdings

Taxes are commonly listed as separate lines on a paystub. The exact taxes shown depend on your location and circumstances.

Paystub item What it generally means
Federal income tax Federal income tax withheld from your pay based on applicable withholding information and rules.
Social Security Employee payroll tax that helps fund Social Security.
Medicare Employee payroll tax associated with Medicare.
State income tax State income-tax withholding where applicable.
Local tax City, county, or other local tax where applicable.

Federal income tax withholding is different from Social Security and Medicare withholding. Seeing several separate tax lines does not mean the same tax is being charged multiple times.

6. Understand Paystub Deductions

Deductions are amounts taken from your earnings for taxes, benefits, retirement contributions, insurance, or other purposes. Not every deduction has the same tax treatment.

Pre-tax deductions

Certain qualifying deductions are taken on a pre-tax basis. Depending on the deduction and the tax involved, this can reduce the amount of wages subject to particular taxes.

Examples can include certain employer health plans, retirement contributions, and eligible flexible spending arrangements.

After-tax deductions

After-tax deductions are generally taken after the applicable taxes have been calculated.

Depending on the employer and plan, examples can include certain Roth retirement contributions, insurance premiums, union dues, charitable contributions, or wage garnishments.

Do not assume every benefit is pre-tax. The tax treatment depends on the specific benefit, payroll arrangement, and applicable rules.

7. What Does YTD Mean on a Paystub?

YTD means year-to-date. It represents cumulative amounts from the beginning of the year through the current paycheck.

Your paystub may show current-period and YTD amounts side by side.

Item Current YTD
Gross pay $2,150 $38,700
Federal tax $210 $3,780
Social Security $133.30 $2,399.40
Medicare $31.18 $561.24
Net pay $1,685.52 $30,339.36

The current column describes this particular paycheck. The YTD column accumulates amounts across the year.

8. Find Your Net Pay

Net pay is commonly called take-home pay. It is the amount remaining after the applicable taxes and deductions are subtracted from your earnings.

Gross Pay − Taxes − Deductions = Net Pay Actual payroll calculations can be more detailed depending on taxable wages and deduction treatment.

If you receive direct deposit, the net-pay amount may be split between multiple bank accounts depending on your direct-deposit instructions.

For budgeting purposes, net pay is generally the figure that matters most because it represents the amount actually available from that paycheck.

9. Check Employer-Paid Benefits

Some paystubs display benefits paid partly or entirely by your employer. These amounts can represent part of your compensation even though they do not necessarily reduce the amount deposited into your bank account.

Depending on the employer, a paystub may show items such as employer retirement contributions, health insurance costs, or employer-paid life insurance.

Employer-paid does not automatically mean take-home pay. These amounts may be useful for understanding your total compensation, but they are different from the net-pay amount.

How Does a Paystub Calculate Take-Home Pay?

The exact payroll calculation varies by employee, but the general process can be understood as a sequence.

1. Earnings Regular pay, overtime, bonuses and other compensation.
2. Gross pay Total earnings before applicable taxes and deductions.
3. Taxable wages Wage amounts used for particular tax calculations.
4. Withholdings Federal, state, local and payroll taxes where applicable.
5. Other deductions Benefits, retirement, insurance and other deductions.
6. Net pay The amount actually paid to the employee.
Earnings → Gross Pay → Tax Calculations → Deductions → Net Pay

What Should You Check on Every Paystub?

Reviewing your paystub can help you catch payroll mistakes before they become difficult to trace.

  • Hours: Do the regular and overtime hours match your records?
  • Pay rate: Is your hourly rate or salary amount correct?
  • Overtime: If you worked overtime, is the additional pay included?
  • Bonuses and commissions: Were expected additional earnings included?
  • Benefits: Are health insurance and other benefit deductions what you expected?
  • Retirement: Does the contribution match your selected amount or percentage?
  • Taxes: Are the withholding lines present and consistent with your circumstances?
  • YTD totals: Do cumulative amounts make sense compared with previous paystubs?
  • Net pay: Does the final amount match the amount actually deposited or paid?

What to Do if Your Paystub Looks Wrong

If something does not match your records, first compare the current paystub with previous statements and your time or earnings records.

  1. Identify the specific number that appears incorrect.
  2. Compare it with your previous paystub or time records.
  3. Check whether a recent change in pay, benefits, tax withholding, or deductions explains the difference.
  4. Contact your employer's payroll or HR department if the discrepancy remains unexplained.
  5. Keep copies of relevant records in case the issue needs additional follow-up.
Do not ignore repeated discrepancies. Small errors can affect multiple pay periods, so unexplained differences are worth investigating promptly.

Why Keep Your Paystubs?

Paystubs can be useful beyond checking whether your paycheck is correct. They provide a record of earnings and deductions during the year.

Budgeting Use net pay to understand the amount available from each paycheck.
Income verification Paystubs may be requested when verifying employment income for certain applications.
Tax records YTD earnings and withholding information can help you monitor payroll tax withholding.
Payroll disputes Previous paystubs can help compare hours, earnings, deductions and YTD amounts.
Protect your personal information. Paystubs can contain sensitive information such as employee identifiers, earnings, deductions and tax information. Avoid posting an unredacted paystub publicly.

How to Read a Paystub: FAQs

A paystub is a statement that explains how your paycheck was calculated. It commonly shows earnings, taxes, deductions, net pay, and year-to-date totals.
Gross pay is the amount earned before applicable taxes and other deductions are subtracted. It can include regular wages, overtime, bonuses, commissions, and other compensation.
YTD means year-to-date. It represents the cumulative amount from the beginning of the year through the current paycheck.
Net pay is the amount remaining after applicable taxes and deductions are subtracted from earnings. It is commonly called take-home pay.
Taxable wages can differ from gross pay because certain deductions and benefits can receive different tax treatment. A paystub may show different wage bases for different taxes.
Check your pay period, pay date, hours, pay rates, earnings, taxes, deductions, taxable wages when shown, YTD totals, and final net pay.
Your paystub may show a net-pay amount that is then distributed through direct deposit according to your payroll instructions. If the amount deposited appears inconsistent with the paystub, check whether your payment was split between accounts or contact payroll.
