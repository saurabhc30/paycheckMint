Gross Pay vs. Net Pay: What's the Difference?

Gross pay is what you earn before payroll deductions. Net pay is what remains after applicable taxes and other deductions are taken out. Learn how gross pay and net pay work, how they appear on a paycheck, and why your take-home pay can be different from your salary.

Quick answer

Gross pay is your total earnings before taxes and other payroll deductions. Net pay is the amount left after applicable deductions and is commonly called your take-home pay.

What Is Gross Pay?

Gross pay is the total amount you earn before taxes and other deductions are taken from your paycheck. It can include your regular wages or salary as well as applicable overtime, bonuses, commissions, and other compensation.

For an hourly employee, gross pay generally starts with the hourly rate multiplied by hours worked. For a salaried employee, gross pay for a paycheck is generally based on annual salary divided across the employee's pay periods.

Hourly gross pay

Hourly Rate × Hours Worked = Regular Gross Pay

If an employee earns $25 per hour and works 40 hours, regular gross pay would be $1,000 before applicable taxes and deductions.

Salary gross pay

Annual Salary ÷ Number of Pay Periods = Regular Gross Pay

For example, a $60,000 annual salary paid biweekly would generally produce 26 regular pay periods:

$60,000 ÷ 26 = $2,307.69

This is gross pay for the regular paycheck before applicable deductions.

What Is Net Pay?

Net pay is the amount that remains after applicable payroll taxes and other deductions are taken from gross pay. It is commonly referred to as take-home pay.

Your net pay is generally the amount shown as the paycheck payment or direct-deposit amount, although the exact terminology and presentation can vary between payroll systems.

Net Pay = Gross Pay − Taxes − Other Deductions

The formula above is a simplified explanation. Actual payroll calculations can involve different tax rules, pre-tax deductions, post-tax deductions, benefits, and other circumstances.

Gross Pay vs. Net Pay

The easiest way to understand the difference is to think of gross pay as the starting amount and net pay as the amount that remains after applicable payroll deductions.

Gross Pay Net Pay
Total earnings before deductions Amount remaining after applicable deductions
Includes regular wages or salary Represents take-home pay
Can include overtime and bonuses Can be reduced by taxes and other deductions
Starting point for many payroll calculations Amount generally available to receive or deposit

How Gross Pay Becomes Net Pay

A paycheck can be viewed as a sequence that starts with gross earnings and ends with the employee's net pay.

Not every paycheck contains every type of deduction. Which deductions apply depends on the employee, employer, benefits, location, elections, and other circumstances.

What Can Be Deducted From Gross Pay?

Several types of deductions can reduce the amount that eventually becomes net pay.

Payroll taxes

  • Federal income tax withholding
  • Social Security tax
  • Medicare tax
  • State income tax where applicable
  • Local income taxes where applicable

Employee benefits

  • Health insurance premiums
  • Dental insurance
  • Vision insurance
  • Other employer-sponsored benefits

Retirement and other deductions

  • 401(k) or other retirement contributions
  • HSA or FSA contributions
  • Eligible voluntary deductions
  • Certain wage garnishments or other required deductions

Pre-Tax vs. Post-Tax Deductions

One important part of understanding a paycheck is knowing that deductions do not necessarily affect the paycheck in exactly the same way.

Some deductions can be taken on a pre-tax basis, depending on the type of deduction and applicable rules. Other deductions are taken after applicable taxes have been calculated.

Why this matters: A $100 deduction does not necessarily reduce every employee's take-home pay by exactly the same amount. The treatment of the deduction can affect the applicable tax calculation.

Gross Pay vs. Net Pay Example

Suppose an employee has gross pay of $2,500 for a paycheck. Assume the employee has $500 in total taxes and other deductions for this simplified example.

$2,500 Gross Pay − $500 Deductions = $2,000 Net Pay

The employee's gross pay is $2,500, while the simplified take-home amount is $2,000.

Actual payroll calculations are more complicated because withholding and deductions depend on the employee's circumstances.

Why Can Two People With the Same Salary Have Different Net Pay?

Two employees can have the same gross salary but receive different amounts in their paychecks.

This can happen because their payroll deductions and withholding elections are different.

  • Different federal tax withholding
  • Different state or local tax situations
  • Different health insurance premiums
  • Different retirement contributions
  • Different HSA or FSA contributions
  • Different voluntary deductions
  • Other applicable payroll deductions

Therefore, salary alone does not tell you exactly how much money will appear in your bank account.

How Pay Frequency Affects Gross and Net Pay

Your annual salary does not change simply because you are paid on a different schedule, but the amount of each individual paycheck does.

Pay Frequency Typical Pay Periods Per Year
Weekly 52
Biweekly 26
Semimonthly 24
Monthly 12

For example, an employee earning $52,000 per year would generally have a different regular gross paycheck under a biweekly schedule than under a semimonthly schedule.

Biweekly and semimonthly are different. Biweekly pay generally occurs every two weeks and typically results in 26 pay periods. Semimonthly pay generally occurs twice per month and typically results in 24 pay periods.

How Overtime Changes Gross and Net Pay

Overtime can increase an hourly employee's gross pay for a paycheck.

For example, if an employee earns $20 per hour and receives an applicable overtime rate of 1.5 times the regular rate, the overtime rate would be:

$20 × 1.5 = $30 per overtime hour

If the employee works five overtime hours, those additional earnings increase gross pay. The employee will not necessarily receive the entire additional gross amount as net pay because applicable taxes and deductions can also affect the paycheck.

What Happens to Bonuses and Commissions?

Bonuses and commissions can increase an employee's gross earnings. The amount ultimately received can be lower after applicable withholding and deductions.

This is one reason a bonus can appear much larger on a paycheck's earnings section than the amount that is ultimately deposited into a bank account.

Where Do Gross Pay and Net Pay Appear on a Pay Stub?

Your pay stub normally provides information about your earnings, taxes, deductions, and the amount paid to you. The exact layout and terminology depend on your employer and payroll provider.

Simplified Pay Stub Example
Gross Pay $2,500
Federal Tax − $250
Social Security − $155
Medicare − $36
Health Insurance − $75
Retirement Contribution − $100
Net Pay $1,884

The numbers above are only an illustrative example and do not represent a standard withholding amount.

Why Is Net Pay More Useful for Personal Budgeting?

Gross pay tells you how much you earn before deductions, but net pay is generally closer to the amount you actually have available from a paycheck for expenses and savings.

When creating a household budget, people commonly need to consider their actual take-home pay rather than simply multiplying their annual salary.

Your budget can then account for expenses such as housing, food, transportation, insurance, debt payments, savings, and other spending.

Gross Pay vs. Gross Income

The terms gross pay and gross income can overlap, but they are not always used in exactly the same way.

Gross pay commonly describes an employee's earnings for a particular paycheck or pay period. Gross income can refer more broadly to income before applicable deductions over a period of time.

For example, an employee could receive $2,000 of gross pay every two weeks. Over 26 pay periods, that would represent $52,000 in gross annual pay.

Net Pay vs. Net Income

Net pay and net income are related terms but can have different meanings depending on context.

In a paycheck context, net pay usually refers to the amount left after applicable payroll deductions and is commonly called take-home pay.

Net income can have a broader personal finance or accounting meaning. If you want to understand that term in more detail, see our guide: What Is Net Income?

Is Gross Pay the Same as Taxable Income?

Not necessarily. Gross pay is the employee's earnings before deductions, while taxable wages or income used for a particular tax calculation can depend on the type of compensation, applicable exclusions, and qualifying deductions.

This is why looking only at a gross-pay number may not tell you exactly how much income will be subject to every tax or withholding calculation.

How to Calculate Net Pay From Gross Pay

A simplified way to think about the calculation is:

Net Pay = Gross Pay − Applicable Taxes − Other Deductions

A practical calculation can follow these steps:

  1. Determine the employee's gross pay for the pay period.
  2. Identify applicable pre-tax deductions.
  3. Determine applicable payroll tax withholding.
  4. Identify applicable post-tax deductions.
  5. Subtract applicable taxes and deductions from gross pay.
  6. The remaining amount is the employee's net pay.

The actual calculation should account for the employee's specific withholding and deduction circumstances.

Why Is My Net Pay Different From My Salary?

Your salary is generally a gross compensation figure, while your paycheck's net pay is the amount remaining after applicable deductions.

For example, someone earning a $60,000 annual salary will not normally receive $60,000 directly in their bank account. The annual salary represents gross compensation, while the actual money received over the year is affected by taxes, benefits, retirement contributions, and other deductions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is gross pay before taxes?

Generally, yes. Gross pay is the employee's earnings before payroll taxes and other deductions are taken from the paycheck.

Is net pay the same as take-home pay?

In a typical paycheck context, yes. Net pay is the amount remaining after applicable taxes and deductions and is commonly called take-home pay.

How do I calculate net pay from gross pay?

A simplified calculation is gross pay minus applicable taxes and other deductions. Actual payroll calculations depend on the employee's withholding and deduction circumstances.

Why is my net pay lower than my gross pay?

Taxes and other applicable deductions are withheld from gross pay before the remaining amount is paid to the employee.

Can two people with the same salary have different net pay?

Yes. Different tax withholding, benefits, retirement contributions, state or local taxes, and other deductions can result in different net pay.

What is the difference between biweekly and semimonthly pay?

Biweekly pay generally occurs 26 times per year, while semimonthly pay generally occurs 24 times per year.

Does overtime increase gross pay?

Yes. Applicable overtime earnings increase gross pay. The resulting net pay depends on taxes and other deductions.

Where can I find gross pay and net pay on my pay stub?

Your pay stub normally identifies earnings, deductions, and a net-pay or take-home amount. The exact layout varies by employer and payroll provider.
