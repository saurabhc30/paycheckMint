What Is Take-Home Pay?

Take-home pay is the amount of money you actually receive from your paycheck after applicable taxes, payroll withholdings, and other deductions are taken from your gross pay. Learn what take-home pay means, how to calculate it, and why it can differ from your salary.

Calculate Your Take-Home Pay Learn How It Works
Quick answer

Take-home pay is the amount remaining from your gross wages after applicable taxes, payroll withholdings, and other deductions are taken out. It is commonly called net pay.

If an employer offers you a $60,000 annual salary, you generally will not receive $60,000 as spendable cash during the year. Salary is usually discussed as a gross compensation amount before payroll deductions.

Your actual paychecks can be reduced by federal income tax withholding, Social Security, Medicare, state or local taxes, retirement contributions, insurance premiums, and other applicable deductions.

The amount left after those deductions is your take-home pay.

What Does Take-Home Pay Mean?

Take-home pay means the amount of money an employee receives after applicable payroll taxes, withholding, and other deductions have been subtracted from gross pay.

On a paycheck or pay stub, this amount is commonly shown as net pay. It represents the amount that is actually paid to the employee for that pay period.

Your take-home pay depends on more than your salary. Pay frequency, withholding information, location, retirement contributions, insurance, overtime, bonuses, and other payroll details can affect the amount of each paycheck.

Take-Home Pay vs. Gross Pay

Gross pay and take-home pay describe different points in the paycheck calculation.

Term Meaning
Gross pay Earnings before applicable payroll taxes and deductions.
Taxable wages Wages used for a particular tax or withholding calculation. These can differ from gross pay depending on applicable rules.
Net pay The amount remaining after applicable payroll taxes and deductions.
Take-home pay A common term for the amount actually received after applicable deductions.

For example, an employee could have $5,000 of gross pay for a pay period but receive less than $5,000 after taxes and other deductions.

Is Take-Home Pay the Same as Net Pay?

In everyday payroll terminology, take-home pay and net pay generally refer to the amount remaining after applicable taxes and deductions are taken from gross pay.

You may also see terms such as net wages or net salary. Payroll systems can use different labels, but the basic concept is the amount left after applicable deductions.

How to Calculate Take-Home Pay

To calculate take-home pay, start with gross pay for the relevant pay period and account for the taxes and deductions that apply to your situation.

Take-Home Pay = Gross Pay − Taxes − Other Deductions This is a simplified formula. Actual payroll calculations can use different taxable wage bases and deduction rules.

Step 1: Determine your gross pay

Gross pay is the amount earned before payroll deductions. For salaried employees, pay-period gross pay generally comes from dividing annual salary across the employer's scheduled pay periods.

For hourly employees, gross pay generally starts with the hourly rate multiplied by hours worked. Overtime, bonuses, commissions, and other compensation can increase gross wages.

Step 2: Account for applicable deductions

Some deductions can be taken from wages before certain taxes are calculated, while others are taken after applicable taxes. The treatment depends on the particular benefit or deduction.

Step 3: Account for federal income tax

Federal income tax withholding depends on factors such as wages and the information an employee provides to the employer on Form W-4. It is not simply one universal percentage of salary.

Step 4: Account for Social Security and Medicare

Social Security and Medicare are payroll taxes generally withheld from covered wages. The applicable calculation can depend on wage levels and payroll rules.

Step 5: Account for state and local taxes

Depending on where you live and work, your paycheck may include state or local income tax withholding. State and local rules vary, so employees with the same gross salary can have different take-home pay.

Step 6: Subtract other deductions

Other deductions can include retirement contributions, health insurance, dental and vision benefits, flexible spending accounts, garnishments, and other payroll deductions.

Step 7: Find your estimated net pay

After the applicable taxes and deductions have been accounted for, the remaining amount is the estimated take-home pay for that paycheck.

What Deductions Reduce Take-Home Pay?

Several types of taxes and deductions can reduce the amount you receive in your paycheck.

Federal income tax

Federal income tax withholding is based on wages and applicable withholding information.

Social Security

Social Security payroll tax is generally withheld from covered employee wages.

Medicare

Medicare tax is generally withheld from covered wages.

State & local taxes

State and local income taxes can reduce take-home pay where applicable.

Retirement contributions

Contributions to retirement plans can reduce the amount available in your paycheck.

Health benefits

Employee-paid health, dental, vision, and other benefits can affect net pay.

Pre-Tax vs. Post-Tax Deductions

Not every payroll deduction is treated the same way for tax purposes. Understanding the difference can help explain why a deduction does not always reduce every type of taxable wage by the same amount.

Type General idea Examples
Pre-tax May be deducted before certain taxes are calculated, depending on the plan and applicable rules. Some traditional retirement, health, HSA, or FSA arrangements.
Tax withholding Amounts withheld from wages to cover applicable taxes. Federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and applicable state taxes.
Post-tax Deductions taken after applicable taxes have been calculated. Certain benefits, insurance, or other payroll deductions.

The exact treatment depends on the specific deduction and payroll arrangement. For that reason, a take-home pay estimate should use the deductions that actually apply to the employee.

Why Is Take-Home Pay Lower Than Salary?

Salary is usually quoted as a gross amount, while take-home pay is the amount left after applicable payroll deductions.

For example, an employee earning a $60,000 annual salary does not normally receive $60,000 of spendable cash directly through their bank account during the year.

Their paychecks may be reduced by:

  • Federal income tax withholding
  • Social Security tax
  • Medicare tax
  • State or local taxes where applicable
  • Retirement contributions
  • Health insurance premiums
  • Other applicable payroll deductions

The difference between gross salary and take-home pay varies from one employee to another.

Take-Home Pay Calculation Example

Consider a simplified example where an employee earns $5,000 in gross pay for a pay period.

Suppose the employee has $700 in combined tax withholding and $300 in other deductions.

Simplified example
Gross pay $5,000
Taxes and withholding − $700
Other deductions − $300
Estimated take-home pay $4,000
This is a simplified educational example, not a tax estimate for a specific employee. Actual payroll taxes and deductions can produce a different result.

The simplified calculation is:

$5,000 − $700 − $300 = $4,000

An actual paycheck calculation can be more detailed because different taxes and deductions can use different rules.

How Pay Frequency Affects Take-Home Pay

Your annual salary does not automatically change because you are paid weekly, biweekly, semimonthly, or monthly. However, the amount of gross and net pay appearing on each paycheck does change.

Pay frequency Typical paychecks per year
Weekly 52
Biweekly 26
Semimonthly 24
Monthly 12

For example, a $60,000 annual salary divided across 26 biweekly pay periods produces approximately $2,307.69 of gross pay per paycheck before taxes and deductions.

Actual take-home pay depends on the applicable withholding and deductions for each pay period.

Where Can You Find Take-Home Pay on a Pay Stub?

Your pay stub normally separates earnings from taxes and other deductions. Depending on the payroll system, you may see labels such as:

  • Gross pay
  • Federal income tax
  • Social Security
  • Medicare
  • State tax
  • Health insurance
  • Retirement contributions
  • Other deductions
  • Net pay

The net pay amount is generally the figure representing the amount actually paid to you for that paycheck.

What Can Change Your Take-Home Pay?

Your take-home pay can change even when your base salary stays the same.

  • Pay frequency: The payroll schedule affects the amount on each paycheck.
  • Form W-4 information: Federal income tax withholding can depend on the information provided to your employer.
  • State and local taxes: Your location can affect paycheck deductions.
  • Retirement contributions: Changing your retirement contribution can change the amount available in your paycheck.
  • Health benefits: Employee-paid insurance premiums can reduce take-home pay.
  • Overtime: Additional hours can increase gross wages and affect withholding.
  • Bonuses: Additional compensation can change the amount paid during a particular pay period.
  • Other deductions: Certain benefits, garnishments, or voluntary deductions can affect net pay.

How to Calculate Take-Home Pay From an Annual Salary

If you know your annual salary, you can first convert it into gross pay for your employer's pay schedule.

Gross Pay Per Paycheck = Annual Salary ÷ Number of Paychecks Per Year

For example, a $60,000 salary paid biweekly has approximately 26 pay periods:

$60,000 ÷ 26 = $2,307.69

That $2,307.69 is the approximate gross paycheck, not the final take-home amount. Taxes and applicable deductions still need to be accounted for.
