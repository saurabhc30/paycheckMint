88% of Americans Plan to Shop Black Friday Weekend—While 1 in 4 Are Still Paying Off Last Year’s Holiday Debt

By PaycheckMint · Published November 26, 2026 · 5 min read

It’s Thanksgiving evening. The leftovers are in the fridge, the deals are already live, and your phone keeps lighting up with doorbusters. New surveys say almost everyone is about to join in. They also say a lot of those shoppers haven’t finished paying for last year.

The short version

  • About 88% of U.S. adults plan to shop at some point between Thanksgiving Day and Cyber Monday, according to a 2026 ICSC survey.
  • Estimates of how many people still owe on last year’s holiday spending run from 6% to 66%. Most mainstream surveys land near 1 in 4.
  • Carrying a balance at credit card rates can wipe out much of a sale’s savings.
  • Buy now, pay later is where the carryover is concentrated: 53% of its users expect to still be paying after January, versus 16% of other shoppers.

Who is shopping this weekend

The headline number depends on how the question is asked, but every major survey points the same way: most Americans plan to shop.

SurveyWhat it foundSample
ICSC, Nov. 9–11, 202688% of U.S. adults (about 237 million) plan to shop from Thanksgiving Day through Cyber Monday. 79% plan to shop on Black Friday.1,007 adults
NRF / Prosper Insights, Oct. 1–7, 2026An estimated 182.7 million consumers plan to shop Thanksgiving weekend, down 2% from 186.9 million in 2025. Black Friday is the most popular day, at 69% of holiday shoppers.8,094 adults

The two surveys measure different things: ICSC counts U.S. adults and the five-day period, while NRF counts holiday shoppers across Thanksgiving weekend. That’s why the percentages don’t match.

How many still owe on last year

This is where the numbers split. The 66% figure that’s been circulating comes from a National Debt Relief survey of 2,000 adults, as reported by The Independent. The press release’s own headline refers to Americans in debt, so it appears to describe people already carrying debt rather than every shopper. Other surveys, which ask all shoppers, find a smaller share.

Different surveys ask different questions of different people, so the results aren’t directly comparable. Treat them as a range: somewhere between a small minority and a clear majority of those already in debt.

Looking ahead, Omni Calculator found 22% of shoppers expect to still be paying off this year’s Black Friday purchases after January.

Why the sale math breaks

Deals are often smaller than people remember. Drive Research’s 2026 survey report says most categories peak at around 20% to 30% off, well short of the 50% many shoppers picture. In an Omni Calculator survey, about 6 in 10 shoppers said they think most Black Friday deals aren’t real savings.

A simple example

A $625 item is 20% off on Black Friday, so you pay $500 and save $125. You put it on a credit card at about 21% interest, the average rate reported for 2025, and let the balance sit for a year.

Interest: up to about $106 on that $500, if you never pay it down.

$125 saved − $106 interest ≈ $19

Paying the balance down each month would cut the interest, but the longer it lingers, the more of the discount disappears. This is a simplified illustration, not your exact rate.

7 steps before you tap Buy

  1. Look up what you still owe. Log in and write down your card balances and interest rates, so tonight’s spending starts from the real number.
  2. Set one total cap. Base it on money you have, not on your credit limit, and write it down before you open an app.
  3. Pay with something you’ll clear in full. A debit card or a credit card you’ll pay off at the statement keeps the sale price the real price.
  4. Go carefully with buy now, pay later. Each plan is a separate bill with its own due date. Stacking several is how small purchases turn into a big monthly total.
  5. Check the real discount. Compare with the price before the sale started. If a “50% off” tag is really 20% off, decide whether you’d still buy it.
  6. Sleep on big purchases. Put it in the cart and wait 24 hours. Many deals run through Cyber Monday.
  7. Plan the January payment. Know which paycheck will cover it before you buy, not after the statement arrives.

If you’re already carrying a balance

You’re not alone, and you have options. Put any extra cash toward the balance with the highest interest rate while paying at least the minimum on the rest. Call your card issuer and ask whether a lower rate or a hardship plan is available. A 0% balance transfer card can help, but these usually charge a fee (often around 3% to 5% of the amount moved) and require good credit, so run the numbers first. If the debt feels unmanageable, a nonprofit credit counseling agency, such as an NFCC member, can review your options for free or at low cost.

Quick answers

Is Black Friday weekend still worth shopping?

It can be, for planned purchases with real discounts that you can pay for in full. Surveys suggest many deals are shallower than shoppers remember, so compare against the price before the sale and skip anything you wouldn’t have bought at full price.

Is buy now, pay later a bad idea?

Not by itself, since some plans charge no interest. The risk is stacking several plans and losing track of due dates. In one 2026 survey, 53% of buy now, pay later users expected to still be paying after January, compared with 16% of other shoppers.

What if I’m still paying off last year’s holiday balance?

Look up the balance and interest rate, then decide on a cap for this year that doesn’t add to it. Put any extra money toward the highest-rate balance, and ask your card issuer or a nonprofit credit counselor about lower-rate options.
