Why a Year-End Bonus or Roth Conversion Can Trigger a $5,900+ Tax & Medicare Hit

By PaycheckMint · Published November 26, 2026 · 6 min read

You open the bonus, or you convert a chunk of your IRA to a Roth to “get ahead on taxes.” It feels responsible. Then, two years later, a letter from Social Security says your Medicare premium just jumped by hundreds of dollars a month. Nothing went wrong. You simply crossed a line you didn’t know was there.

The short version

  • The extra charge is called IRMAA, the income-related monthly adjustment amount. Technically it’s a surcharge on Medicare Part B and Part D, not a tax or a penalty, but it feels like both.
  • Medicare looks back two years. Your 2026 income (including a December bonus or conversion) sets your 2028 premiums. Your 2027 premiums are based on 2025.
  • The tiers are cliffs. One dollar over a line moves you to the next tier for the whole year.
  • A Roth conversion can’t be undone, and a conversion isn’t a reason Social Security will let you appeal.

Where the $5,900+ comes from

The figure isn’t a flat fee. It depends on which tier you land in and whether two spouses are both paying. Here are the official 2027 tiers announced by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) on Nov. 17, 2026. The standard Part B premium is $209.40 a month, and the surcharge is on top of it. The last column adds Part B and Part D together for one person over a full year.

PaycheckMint calculation from the CMS 2027 figures: (Part B tier − $209.40 + Part D surcharge) × 12. Part D applies only if you’re enrolled in a Part D plan. Income here means modified adjusted gross income (MAGI): your adjusted gross income plus tax-exempt interest.

So where does $5,900+ come in? A married couple who both pay Part B and Part D and land in the $282,000 tier pay $5,025.60 in extra Part B plus $952.80 in extra Part D. That’s $5,978.40 a year more than a couple under the first line. Higher tiers and single filers at the upper end go past that. Lower tiers cost less, but even the first step adds $2,008.80 a year in Part B for a couple.

The timeline that catches people off guard

  • Dec. 31, 2026. The bonus lands, or the Roth conversion is completed. Conversions have to be finished by year-end to count for 2026.
  • Spring 2027. You file your 2026 return and owe regular income tax on the extra income.
  • 2028. Social Security uses that 2026 return to set your Part B and Part D surcharges for the year.

One caveat: the 2028 tier lines haven’t been published yet. CMS adjusts most of them each year, and this year’s moved up by roughly 3%, so the 2027 table above is the best guide available. Treat any number that’s close to a line as a maybe.

What a $15,000 bonus can really cost

An illustrative couple, both on Medicare in 2028

Their income would be $270,000 without the bonus. A $15,000 year-end bonus takes them to $285,000, which is over the $282,000 line.

Medicare: Part B goes up $3,016.80 and Part D goes up $583.20 for the two of them, compared with the tier below.

Income tax: about $3,600 on the bonus, assuming a 24% federal bracket and ignoring state tax.

≈ $7,200 on a $15,000 bonus

That’s roughly 48 cents of every bonus dollar, and most of it arrives two years after the money does. This is a simplified example using 2027 tier lines. Your numbers will differ.

Your employer’s payroll withholding won’t warn you. Bonuses are typically withheld at a flat 22% federal supplemental rate (37% on the part over $1 million), which says nothing about Medicare.

Who actually needs to worry

Mostly anyone who will be on Medicare in 2028, which means roughly age 63 or older today. People who are still a few years from Medicare usually aren’t affected yet, though a very large one-time income year can still matter later. IRMAA only applies for the months you have coverage, so it’s about when you’ll be enrolled, not just your age.

Moves worth checking before Dec. 31

  1. Find your line first. Estimate your 2026 MAGI and see how far you are from the next tier in the table. If you’re $8,000 below it, a $15,000 conversion is the problem. If you’re already well past it, the extra step may matter less.
  2. Shrink or split the Roth conversion. Converting just enough to stay under a line, then doing more next year, can avoid the cliff. Keep in mind that 2027 income will set 2029 premiums, so spreading doesn’t make the lookback disappear.
  3. Ask about timing the bonus. Some employers allow a bonus to be paid in January or deferred into a retirement plan. Elections like that generally have to be made before the income is earned, so ask HR now.
  4. Lower your income with the tools you have. Pre-tax retirement contributions, charitable giving, and, if you’re 70½ or older, qualified charitable distributions from an IRA can all reduce MAGI. HSA contributions can too, but not once you’re enrolled in Medicare.
  5. Run it by a tax pro. The right answer depends on your bracket, state, and how much you’d save in future taxes by converting now. A bigger Medicare bill isn’t always a reason to skip the conversion.

Can you fight it later?

Sometimes. If your income dropped because of a listed life-changing event, such as retirement, reduced work hours, or the death of a spouse, you can ask Social Security to use a more recent tax year with Form SSA-44. A one-time income spike, like a Roth conversion or a bonus, isn’t on the list. That’s why the decision is best made now.

The bottom line

A bonus or a Roth conversion can be a smart move, and sometimes paying a higher premium for a year is still the better deal. The point is to make the choice with the number in front of you, not to find out from a letter in 2028. Before Dec. 31, find your line, see how close you are, and decide on purpose.

Quick answers

Is IRMAA a tax?

No. It’s an extra amount added to your Medicare Part B and Part D premiums based on your income. It’s collected through those premiums, often deducted from Social Security.

Does this apply if I’m not on Medicare yet?

Only once you’re enrolled. But if you’ll be on Medicare in 2028, your 2026 income is the one that counts.

Does a Roth conversion count as income?

Yes. A conversion from a pre-tax account is taxable income in the year you do it, and it raises your MAGI. Withdrawals from a Roth IRA later generally don’t.

Can I undo a Roth conversion if I cross a line?

No. Conversions can no longer be recharacterized, so the decision is final once it’s done.
