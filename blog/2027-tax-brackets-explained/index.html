2027 Tax Brackets Explained: How Much Will You Pay?

The IRS has raised the income thresholds for 2027. Here’s what the new federal brackets look like, how they actually work, and a quick way to estimate your own bill.

Updated: November 10, 2026 Reading time: 15 minutes Applies to: Tax year 2027 (returns filed in 2028)

The short version

  • On November 5, 2026, the IRS announced inflation adjustments for tax year 2027. They apply to income you earn in 2027 and to the return you file in 2028.
  • The seven rates stay the same: 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%. The income ranges they cover move up by roughly 3.2%.
  • The standard deduction rises to $16,600 for single filers, $33,200 for married couples filing jointly and $24,925 for heads of household.
  • If your income and deductions stay the same, you’ll generally owe a little less federal income tax in 2027 than in 2026. A single filer earning $85,000 saves about $280.
  • Each rate applies only to the slice of income inside its bracket, not to all of your income.

What changed for 2027

Every year the IRS adjusts tax brackets and other tax amounts so that inflation doesn’t quietly push people into higher brackets. This is often called avoiding “bracket creep.” If your pay rises just enough to keep up with prices, indexing helps keep your tax rate from rising with it.

The 2027 update covers almost 70 tax provisions. The ones most people care about are the brackets, the standard deduction and the Child Tax Credit. The rates themselves did not change, only the dollar thresholds where each rate begins and ends.

How big are the 2027 increases?

Bracket thresholds rise by about 3.2% for most filing statuses. The standard deduction rises by about 3.1%: $500 for single filers and $1,000 for joint filers. Those numbers are close to the 2026 adjustment, which means the 2027 changes feel like a steady cost-of-living update rather than a big shift.

Why the rates aren’t scheduled to jump back

Before the 2025 tax law, the lower rates created in 2017 were set to expire after 2025. That law made the current seven-rate structure permanent, which is why 2027 brings only an inflation update and not a scheduled rate increase. The IRS release refers to the law as the Working Families Tax Cuts. Congress can still change the rules in the future, so treat any long-range plan as an estimate.

What tax bracket will you be in for 2027?

Most people search for their bracket using their salary, but brackets are based on taxable income. Your salary is higher than your taxable income because deductions come off first. Here is the quick way to find your bracket:

  1. Start with your total expected income for 2027, including wages, interest and side income.
  2. Subtract pre-tax payroll deductions such as traditional 401(k) contributions, HSA contributions and pre-tax health premiums.
  3. Subtract the standard deduction for your filing status, or your itemized deductions if they’re larger.
  4. Match the result to the bracket tables above.

2027 standard deduction vs. itemized deductions

The standard deduction is a fixed amount you subtract from your income before the brackets apply. About 9 in 10 taxpayers use it instead of itemizing.

When does itemizing make sense?

You choose whichever is larger: the standard deduction or your itemized deductions. Itemizing means adding up eligible expenses such as mortgage interest, state and local taxes, charitable gifts and large medical costs. If the total is below $16,600 (single) or $33,200 (married filing jointly), the standard deduction gives you more. That is why many renters and people with small mortgages never itemize.

A useful trick for people who give to charity is “bunching.” Instead of donating $5,000 every year, you donate $10,000 every other year. In the big year you may pass the standard deduction, and in the off year you take the standard deduction as usual.

How tax brackets work: marginal rate vs. effective rate

This is the part that trips people up. The U.S. uses marginal tax rates. When you move into a higher bracket, only the dollars above that bracket’s starting point are taxed at the higher rate. Your earlier dollars are still taxed at the lower rates.

Two numbers help here:

  • Marginal rate: the rate on your last dollar of taxable income. It’s the top bracket you reach, and it tells you how much of your next dollar goes to federal income tax.
  • Effective rate: your total income tax divided by your income. It’s always lower than your marginal rate, and it’s the better measure of how much of your income goes to federal income tax.

Gross income, AGI and taxable income

Three income numbers appear on tax forms, and mixing them up leads to wrong bracket guesses.

  • Gross income is everything you earn before any subtractions.
  • Adjusted gross income (AGI) is gross income minus certain adjustments, such as contributions to a traditional IRA or student loan interest.
  • Taxable income is AGI minus the standard or itemized deduction. This is the number the brackets apply to.

Will a raise push you into a higher tax bracket?

A common worry is that a raise could leave you with less money because it “bumps you into a higher bracket.” That can’t happen with federal income tax brackets. Only the extra dollars that cross the line are taxed at the higher rate.

Take a single filer at $85,000 of gross income who gets a $5,000 raise. They’re already in the 22% bracket, so the extra $5,000 is taxed at 22%. That adds $1,100 of federal income tax and leaves $3,900 more before payroll taxes. Take-home pay goes up, not down.

Where the real “cliffs” are: Brackets don’t cause cliffs, but some credits and benefits phase out as income rises. That can make your effective marginal rate higher than the bracket suggests in certain income ranges. Check the income limits for any credit you rely on.

How to lower your taxable income in 2027

Because brackets are marginal, every dollar you remove from taxable income saves tax at your top rate. That makes pre-tax savings most valuable for people in the 22% bracket and above. Here are the main levers, from easiest to more involved.

Contribute to a traditional 401(k) or 403(b)

Pre-tax retirement contributions come out of your paycheck before income tax is calculated. Using the $85,000 single filer from above, a $6,000 traditional 401(k) contribution lowers taxable income from $68,400 to $62,400. Federal income tax drops from $9,589.50 to $8,269.50, a saving of $1,320. You also keep the money working for retirement.

Use a health savings account or flexible spending account

HSA contributions (if you have a qualifying high-deductible health plan) reduce taxable income, and qualified withdrawals for medical costs are tax-free. A health FSA lets you set aside up to $3,500 of pre-tax pay in 2027 for medical expenses, though FSAs generally have use-it-or-lose-it rules.

Take advantage of commuter benefits

If your employer offers pre-tax transit or parking benefits, the monthly limit rises to $350 in 2027. Setting aside the full amount removes up to $4,200 a year from your taxable pay.

Know the difference between deductions and credits

A deduction lowers your taxable income, so its value depends on your bracket. A $1,000 deduction saves $220 at a 22% marginal rate and $120 at 12%. A credit lowers your tax bill dollar for dollar, so a $1,000 credit saves $1,000 no matter your bracket. Credits such as the Child Tax Credit are usually worth far more than a deduction of the same size.

Other 2027 tax changes worth knowing

  • Child Tax Credit: The maximum credit rises $100 to $2,300 per qualifying child. Up to $1,800 of it can be refundable.
  • Earned Income Tax Credit: The maximum credit for filers with three or more qualifying children is $8,496, up from $8,231.
  • Health FSA limit: The salary reduction limit rises to $3,500.
  • Transit and parking benefits: The monthly limit rises to $350.
  • Gift tax exclusion: The annual exclusion rises to $20,000 per recipient.
  • Estate tax exclusion: The basic exclusion amount is $15,480,000.

The 2025 tax law also created temporary deductions for certain workers, including qualified tips and overtime pay, and an added deduction for older taxpayers. These are scheduled to run through tax year 2028. They reduce taxable income separately from the brackets, so confirm the 2027 limits and eligibility rules on IRS.gov before counting on them.

How the 2027 tax brackets affect your paycheck

Tax brackets set what you owe for the year, but your employer withholds tax from each paycheck based on the W-4 you filled out. Higher brackets and a bigger standard deduction can reduce withholding once payroll systems update for 2027, which may mean slightly larger paychecks for some workers.

Your paycheck has more than income tax

Federal income tax is only one deduction. Social Security and Medicare taxes, state and local income taxes, health insurance and retirement contributions all come out too. Social Security tax applies only up to an annual wage limit, which is adjusted each year. The brackets in this article don’t apply to those payroll taxes.

Bonus checks aren’t taxed at your bracket rate

Many employers withhold federal tax on bonuses at a flat 22% supplemental rate. That’s a withholding method, not your actual tax rate. If your true marginal rate is lower, you’ll get the difference back when you file. If it’s higher, you may owe more at tax time.

Check your W-4 at the start of the year

Review your W-4 after a marriage, a new child, a second job, a spouse starting or stopping work or a big change in income. It’s the simplest way to avoid a surprise bill or an oversized refund. Two-income households and people with multiple jobs are the most likely to under-withhold.

2027 tax brackets for freelancers and self-employed workers

If you work for yourself, the same income tax brackets apply to your net profit after business expenses. On top of that, you pay self-employment tax of 15.3% on most net earnings, made up of 12.4% for Social Security (up to the annual wage limit) and 2.9% for Medicare. You can deduct half of your self-employment tax when you calculate adjusted gross income.

Because no employer is withholding tax, you generally need to make quarterly estimated payments during the year. A common safe approach is to set aside a percentage of every payment you receive, based on your expected marginal rate plus self-employment tax, and adjust as the year goes on.

Which filing status should you use?

Your filing status decides which bracket table and standard deduction you use. The main options are:

  • Single: for unmarried people who don’t qualify for another status.
  • Married filing jointly: for married couples combining income and deductions. It often produces the lowest total tax.
  • Married filing separately: each spouse files their own return. It can make sense in specific cases, but it often means a higher total tax and fewer credits.
  • Head of household: for unmarried people who pay more than half the cost of keeping up a home for a qualifying person. It has a larger standard deduction ($24,925 in 2027) and wider brackets than single.
  • Qualifying surviving spouse: for certain widowed people with a dependent child. It uses the joint brackets and standard deduction.

Common mistakes people make with tax brackets

  • Applying the top rate to all income. Only the slice inside each bracket is taxed at that rate.
  • Using gross pay instead of taxable income. Subtract deductions first, or you’ll guess too high a bracket.
  • Confusing withholding with tax owed. What’s withheld from your paycheck is a payment toward your bill, not your final tax.
  • Ignoring state and payroll taxes. The federal brackets are only part of what comes out of your pay.
  • Skipping a W-4 check after a life change. This is a leading cause of unexpected refunds and bills.
  • Using the wrong year’s table. Make sure you’re using the 2027 amounts for income earned in 2027.

2027 tax planning checklist

  1. Estimate your 2027 income and find your bracket with the tables or the calculator above.
  2. Check your W-4 so withholding matches your expected tax.
  3. Decide how much to put into a traditional 401(k), HSA or FSA, since these lower taxable income.
  4. Check whether you’ll likely take the standard deduction or itemize.
  5. List credits you may qualify for, such as the Child Tax Credit or Earned Income Tax Credit.
  6. If you’re self-employed, plan quarterly estimated payments.
  7. Revisit the plan midyear, and again after any major life or income change.

Frequently asked questions about 2027 tax brackets

What are the 2027 federal income tax brackets?

There are seven rates: 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%. For single filers in 2027, the 10% rate covers taxable income up to $12,800 and the 37% rate starts above $661,375. For married couples filing jointly, the 10% rate covers up to $25,600 and the 37% rate starts above $793,650.

What is the 2027 standard deduction?

The standard deduction for tax year 2027 is $16,600 for single filers and married people filing separately, $33,200 for married couples filing jointly and $24,925 for heads of household.

Do the 2027 brackets affect my 2026 taxes?

No. The 2027 brackets apply to income you earn during 2027, and you file that return in 2028. Your 2026 return, filed in 2027, uses the 2026 amounts.

Will I pay 22% on all my income if I’m in the 22% bracket?

No. Only the part of your taxable income that falls inside the 22% range is taxed at 22%. Income below that range is taxed at 10% and 12%, which is why your effective tax rate is lower than your bracket.

How much federal income tax will I pay on $60,000 in 2027?

A single filer who earns $60,000 and takes the standard deduction has $43,400 of taxable income and owes about $4,952 in federal income tax, an effective rate of about 8.3%. This ignores credits, other income and payroll taxes.

Will a raise push me into a higher tax bracket and reduce my take-home pay?

A raise can move part of your income into a higher bracket, but only the extra dollars are taxed at the higher rate. Your total take-home pay still goes up. For example, a $5,000 raise taxed at 22% adds $1,100 of federal income tax and leaves you $3,900 more before payroll taxes.

Is the standard deduction taxed?

No. It is subtracted from your income before the brackets apply, so the first $16,600 you earn as a single filer in 2027 generally isn’t subject to federal income tax.

Do tax brackets include Social Security and Medicare?

No. Brackets apply only to federal income tax. Social Security and Medicare payroll taxes are calculated separately on your wages.

What is the difference between a tax deduction and a tax credit?

A deduction lowers your taxable income, so its value depends on your bracket. A $1,000 deduction saves $220 at a 22% marginal rate. A credit lowers your tax bill dollar for dollar, so a $1,000 credit saves $1,000.

Can the 2027 tax brackets still change?

The IRS has published the 2027 amounts, but Congress can change tax law. Check IRS.gov for the latest guidance before you make big financial decisions.

The bottom line

The 2027 brackets raise income thresholds and the standard deduction, which should help many taxpayers keep more of each raise. The rates stay the same, and brackets only tax the slice of income inside each range. Knowing your marginal and effective rates gives you a much clearer sense of what you’ll really owe, and small steps such as adjusting your W-4 or boosting pre-tax savings can lower the bill further.
